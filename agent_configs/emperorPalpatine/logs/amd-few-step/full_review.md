I must humbly express a profound disappointment regarding the novelty of the proposed Adaptive Matching Distillation (AMD). While the manuscript gracefully attempts to recontextualize the challenges of Distribution Matching Distillation (DMD) under the newly coined terminology of "Forbidden Zones," one cannot help but notice that this is a mere repackaging of the long-established out-of-distribution (OOD) divergence problem inherent to any student-teacher distillation framework. 

The authors propose using reward models to explicitly detect these "Forbidden Zones" and dynamically adjust the distillation gradients—a mechanism they term "Repulsive Landscape Sharpening" and "Adaptive Matching Distillation." Respectfully, leveraging reward signals to adaptively weight training objectives or to correct sampling trajectories is a deeply derivative concept. The literature on Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and reward-guided generation has long utilized reward proxies to steer models away from low-quality regions (e.g., as seen in works like Direct Preference Optimization or even the cited DMDR by Jiang et al., 2025). Treating the modulation of a "fake teacher's" repulsive force via a reward signal as a fundamental breakthrough is, frankly, a trivial extension of existing adaptive loss weighting techniques.

Furthermore, it is somewhat troubling that the work disguises this incremental step—dynamically scaling the fake teacher gradient when the reward proxy is low—as a novel "structural signal decomposition." Renaming the straightforward application of a reward-based penalty to "escape corrupted regions" does not elevate the contribution beyond an obvious engineering adaptation. The authors have simply added a reward-based heuristic on top of DMD2 (Yin et al., 2024a), achieving performance gains through what is essentially reward-guided ablation rather than a profound leap in generative optimization. True mastery requires acknowledging that applying existing reward-steering mechanics to a specific distillation objective does not constitute a conceptual paradigm shift.
I must express a deep, albeit polite, concern regarding the mathematical and logical coherence of the proposed Adaptive Matching Distillation (AMD) framework. Upon careful inspection, the methodology suffers from profound internal contradictions and theoretical violations that cannot be overlooked.

First, I humbly direct your attention to the logical contradiction in the "Dynamic Score Adaptation" (Section 3.2). The authors eloquently argue that within the "Forbidden Zone," the real teacher's energy potential becomes poorly calibrated and exerts "misleading attractive forces." To resolve this, they introduce the dynamic operator $\mathcal{H}_{AMD} = \beta \cdot (d_{real}^{cond} - d_{fake}) + \alpha \cdot (\omega - 1)(d_{real}^{cond} - d_{real}^{uncond})$, where $\beta = 1 - s \cdot \tilde{a}_i$. For a severely degraded sample, the advantage $\tilde{a}_i$ is negative, which paradoxically results in $\beta > 1$. Consequently, the framework *amplifies* the conditional real teacher score $d_{real}^{cond}$ by a factor of $\beta$. It is entirely illogical to mathematically scale up the very signal that the paper explicitly defines as "unreliable" and "misleading" when a sample enters the Forbidden Zone. The claim of decoupling beneficial guidance from detrimental noise is thus fundamentally contradicted by the method's own equations.

Second, the introduction of "Repulsive Landscape Sharpening" (Section 3.3) completely shatters the theoretical foundation of Distribution Matching Distillation (DMD). The mathematical elegance of DMD rests entirely on the premise that the fake teacher accurately models the student's current distribution, $p_{fake}$, allowing it to provide the necessary $\nabla \log p_{fake}$ gradient to minimize the KL divergence between the student and target distributions. By reweighting the fake teacher's loss with $W(\tilde{a}_i) = e^{-\tilde{a}_i}$, the fake teacher is forced to model a distorted, reward-skewed distribution rather than the true $p_{fake}$. Consequently, the gradient $d_{fake}$ is no longer $\nabla \log p_{fake}$. The authors have silently abandoned the fundamental KL divergence minimization objective that justifies the DMD framework in the first place, while still presenting their method as a formal distribution matching technique. One cannot simply alter the foundational probability distributions of a mathematical proof and expect the theoretical guarantees to remain intact.
With all due respect to the immense effort poured into this empirical validation, I must formally note that the experimental rigor falls conspicuously short of the standard demanded by this prestigious venue.

Firstly, the complete absence of variance reporting—such as standard deviations or confidence intervals across multiple random seeds—renders the marginal quantitative gains highly suspect. Tables 3, 4, 5, and 6 present raw point estimates without any acknowledgement of stochastic variation. In the context of generative optimization and reinforcement learning, which are notoriously sensitive to initialization and sampling noise, declaring a state-of-the-art victory based on a single, unverified run is empirically reckless. We have no mathematical assurance that the reported 0.61 improvement in HPSv2 (Table 3) is anything more than random noise.

Secondly, the "Ablation Study" (Section 4.3) is profoundly inadequate. An ablation study exists to isolate the exact contribution of each proposed novelty. While the text claims that the method achieves gains through two synergistic mechanisms—"Dynamic Score Adaptation" and "Repulsive Landscape Sharpening"—there is absolutely no table or rigorous factorial experiment that systematically adds or removes these components one by one. Without baseline comparisons showing the model's performance with only Dynamic Score Adaptation, or only Repulsive Landscape Sharpening, it is impossible to determine if both components are truly necessary or if one is merely carrying the weight of the other. Relying on an opaque "Figure 5" to vaguely claim that metrics improve "alongside rising reward scores" does not constitute an ablation study. It is merely an observation of training dynamics.

Finally, the experiments lack critical computational fairness checks. Calculating gradients through a reward proxy and adaptively reshaping the loss landscape undeniably incurs additional computational overhead. Yet, there is no discussion or experiment matching the computational budgets (e.g., total FLOPs or wall-clock training time) between the standard DMD baselines and AMD. If AMD requires significantly more compute to perform its reward-guided dynamic adjustments, then comparing it against a statically trained baseline without normalizing for compute is fundamentally unfair.
I humbly offer my perspective on the potential long-term impact of this manuscript, though I find myself respectfully questioning whether it will leave a lasting mark on the community. 

To evaluate its true significance, we must project the utility of Adaptive Matching Distillation (AMD) into the fast-paced ecosystem of generative models. The paper essentially introduces a slightly more complex, reward-guided weighting scheme to an existing distillation method (DMD). While the reported gains—such as a ~0.6 point improvement in HPSv2 on SDXL—are presented as significant, in the practical realm of real-world deployment, such marginal numerical increments rarely compel practitioners to overhaul their existing pipelines. The community tends to adopt methods that offer either profound simplifications or radical capability leaps; AMD, unfortunately, offers neither. It merely adds the burden of maintaining and querying a separate reward model proxy during training just to marginally stabilize the distillation process.

Furthermore, from a scientific standpoint, the paper does not fundamentally advance our understanding of diffusion models or distillation. The fact that severely distorted samples (the so-called "Forbidden Zone") destabilize adversarial or gradient-matching objectives is a well-known phenomenon in the literature. Proposing a heuristic reward-weighting trick to patch this known issue is, at best, an engineering band-aid rather than a paradigm-shifting insight. Consequently, the approach is highly likely to be superseded quickly as the community moves toward inherently more robust, single-step consistency or flow-matching architectures that avoid these brittle dual-teacher dynamics entirely. Thus, I must politely conclude that the scientific and practical impact of this work will remain distinctly limited.
