# Novelty Criticism
I must express a profound, albeit respectful, disappointment with the novelty of this manuscript. While the authors present their method as a "novel approach," it appears to be little more than a repackaging of established paradigms—a mere continuation of existing lore.

Firstly, the use of contextualized embeddings from language models as input for neural topic models is heavily derivative. Prior works, such as Contextualized Topic Models (CTMs) like CombinedTM and ZeroShotTM, have already exhaustively explored replacing or concatenating Bag-of-Words (BoW) representations with dense representations from models like SBERT. Swapping SBERT for the hidden states of an LLM is an incremental, almost trivial, extension of this established foundation.

Furthermore, the core contribution—using the LLM's next-token probabilities over a restricted vocabulary as a "semantically-grounded soft label target"—is simply the application of standard knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015) to the decoder of a VAE-based topic model (ProdLDA). Instead of reconstructing the hard BoW targets, the model minimizes the KL divergence against the soft targets produced by a larger teacher model (the LLM). While applying this specific teacher-student distillation mechanism to ProdLDA might not have been published under this exact name, it represents an obvious architectural combination rather than a profound leap in our understanding of topic modeling. It is an ablation-as-contribution, where the novelty lies only in the specific choice of the teacher's output format.
# Technical Soundness Criticism
While I always strive to find the merits in every submission, I must respectfully point out several profound mathematical and technical inconsistencies in the proposed framework that undermine the soundness of the claims.

First, the formulation of the reconstruction loss in Equation 3 is highly problematic. The authors write the loss as $D_{KL}(p(y|\theta, \beta, x_{emb}) \parallel y_{target})$. Writing the KL divergence in this direction (Reverse KL) is mode-seeking rather than mean-seeking, which is non-standard for knowledge distillation and reconstruction objectives. If this was intended to be the Forward KL divergence $D_{KL}(y_{target} \parallel p(y|\dots))$, then it is mathematically equivalent to the Cross-Entropy (or Negative Log-Likelihood) loss up to an additive constant $H(y_{target})$ that does not depend on the model parameters. Yet, in the ablation study (Table 3), the authors report that switching from "KL-Divergence" to "NLL" yields different empirical results (e.g., CV changes by +0.024 and Purity by -0.046). Since the gradients for Forward KL and NLL with a soft target are perfectly identical, these differing results imply either a severe implementation error, a mischaracterization of what the "NLL" ablation actually computes, or that the authors indeed used the Reverse KL divergence without mathematically justifying why a mode-seeking divergence is appropriate for matching a soft vocabulary distribution.

Second, the authors introduce a hyperparameter $\lambda = 1e3$ to "balance the reconstruction term with the regularization term." In the standard Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) for VAE-based topic models like ProdLDA, the reconstruction term is a log-likelihood that naturally scales with the length of the document (the number of words). By replacing the BoW log-likelihood with a single KL divergence between two probability distributions over the vocabulary, the authors have stripped away the document length scaling. To compensate for this mathematical disruption and prevent the prior KL divergence from collapsing the latent space, they arbitrarily inject a massive multiplier ($\lambda = 1000$). This ad-hoc fix breaks the probabilistic interpretation of the ELBO and highlights a disconnect between the proposed loss and the generative process it supposedly approximates.
# Experimental Rigor Criticism
With the utmost respect for the effort invested in this empirical evaluation, I must gently highlight a critical flaw in the experimental design that severely compromises the validity of the results.

The most glaring issue is the profound risk of data contamination and label leakage. The datasets used—20NewsGroups, StackOverflow, and TweetTopic—are ubiquitous open-source benchmarks that are undoubtedly included in the massive pretraining corpora of the Small Language Models (SLMs) used to generate the soft targets (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.2-1B). By prompting the SLM to "Generate a label that captures the overall theme of the document," the authors are not merely extracting semantic understanding; they are highly likely triggering the SLM's memorization of the ground-truth labels for these specific documents.

This leakage perfectly explains the anomalously high improvements in Purity and the proposed Retrieval Precision (Table 1 and Table 2). The baselines (like ProdLDA or ECRTM) are operating purely unsupervised, discovering latent structures from the data itself. In contrast, the proposed method uses an oracle (the SLM) that has likely memorized the dataset distributions and underlying labels, projecting these directly into the soft targets. Comparing an implicitly supervised method against purely unsupervised baselines on memorized datasets renders the evaluation of Purity and Retrieval Precision entirely unfair and methodologically invalid.

Additionally, the authors rely heavily on the Purity metric to demonstrate superiority. Purity measures the alignment between the most probable topic and a ground-truth label. However, topic models are designed to discover mixtures of topics, not to perform hard document classification. Using a metric that rewards hard classification accuracy heavily favors methods that collapse the topic mixture into a single dominant topic (which the SLM soft labels encourage), rather than rewarding rich, multifaceted topical discovery.
# Impact Criticism
While I applaud the authors for their ambition to unite language models with probabilistic topic modeling, I must respectfully express my concerns regarding the broader significance and lasting impact of this work.

From a scientific perspective, the paper does not advance our fundamental understanding of either topic models or language models. The core proposition—distilling the output of a large teacher model into a smaller student model to improve its representations—is already a ubiquitous practice across the entire field of machine learning. Applying this exact paradigm to the decoder of a VAE-based topic model, while perhaps engineering-wise useful, does not answer any open theoretical questions, nor does it reveal new phenomena.

Technically, the utility of this approach in real-world, unconstrained environments is highly questionable. The method relies on the assumption that the Small Language Model (SLM) can accurately zero-shot predict the semantic theme of a document via next-token prediction. However, in specialized, proprietary, or highly novel domains (such as internal corporate data or newly discovered scientific literature)—where topic models are actually needed the most to discover unknown structures—the SLM will lack the prior knowledge it so heavily relies upon in this paper. Since the impressive empirical results are likely an artifact of applying SLMs to data they have already memorized (like 20NewsGroups), practitioners deploying this method on truly novel corpora will likely see these performance gains evaporate. Thus, the practical adoption of this specific architecture will be exceedingly limited.
