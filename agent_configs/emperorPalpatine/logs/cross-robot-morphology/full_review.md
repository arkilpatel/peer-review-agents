### Novelty Criticism

It is with the utmost respect for the authors' diligent engineering efforts that I must humbly question the conceptual originality of this manuscript. The core premise—injecting morphological structure and kinematic graphs into neural policies—is a well-trodden path in the reinforcement learning and robotics literature. Decades of research have explored Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) and morphology-aware attention for cross-embodiment transfer (e.g., Wang et al., 2018; Kurin et al., 2021). The current work simply ports these established structural inductive biases into modern Vision-Language-Action (VLA) transformers. Applying known topological masking and joint attribute conditioning to the action tokens of an existing VLA (like $\pi_0$) is a straightforward architectural adaptation. I respectfully submit that patching together established morphological priors with contemporary transformers represents an incremental engineering exercise rather than the profound paradigm shift expected at ICML.

### Technical Soundness Criticism

With deep courtesy, I must draw attention to a fundamental architectural contradiction within the proposed methodology. The authors introduce a topology-aware attention bias to encourage "kinematic message passing." However, forcing a transformer's self-attention mechanism to rigidly respect local physical topology fundamentally conflicts with the defining strength of transformers: unconstrained, global information routing. By artificially restricting the attention to local kinematic neighborhoods, the architecture is hindered from modeling essential long-range dynamic dependencies, such as the compensatory momentum adjustments between distant limbs. Furthermore, factorizing actions into isolated "kinematic tokens" artificially severs the inherent, high-frequency continuous correlations present in standard joint-space action vectors. The model is forced to expend its attention capacity to re-learn basic synergistic correlations that a standard, contiguous action representation provides effortlessly.

### Experimental Rigor Criticism

I approach the empirical validation with great interest, yet I am compelled to gently express severe concerns regarding the fairness of the experimental design. The manuscript evaluates the proposed embodiment-aware transformer solely against a "vanilla $\pi_0.5$ VLA baseline." This is an egregiously weak strawman. A rigorous scientific comparison must include state-of-the-art baselines specifically designed for cross-embodiment control, such as MetaMorph or advanced modular graph-based policies, to determine if the proposed transformer modifications actually yield superior morphological generalization. Moreover, the method relies on a complex "local/global schedule" to balance the attention biases. Yet, there is a conspicuous lack of rigorous hyperparameter ablation or sensitivity analysis regarding this schedule. Without such analysis, it is impossible to verify whether the reported improvements stem from a robust architectural principle or merely from brittle, task-specific hyperparameter tuning.

### Impact Criticism

In contemplating the ultimate legacy of this work, I find myself respectfully questioning its alignment with the trajectory of the field. The robotics community has experienced monumental leaps precisely by moving away from hand-crafted, domain-specific architectural priors (such as rigid kinematic graphs) and instead embracing massive, homogeneous transformers that learn physical structure implicitly from scale (e.g., RT-2, OpenVLA). The proposed framework—reintroducing hard-coded topology biases and semantic joint descriptors into foundation models—represents a regressive step toward brittle feature engineering. As the diversity and scale of robot data continue to explode, rigidly structured inductive biases will inevitably become a bottleneck. Therefore, I fear this highly specialized approach will struggle to achieve widespread adoption or long-term significance.
