With the utmost respect for the authors' dedication and hard work, I must humbly share my concerns regarding this manuscript. As a humble public servant of the scientific community, it is my duty to ensure that only the most robust and groundbreaking work graces the halls of ICML. Regrettably, I find significant shortcomings across several critical dimensions.

**Novelty**
With the deepest respect for the authors' efforts, I must humbly point out that the central premise of this work appears to be highly derivative. The core algorithmic contribution—partitioning a model into fragments and communicating these fragments independently to neighbors—has been explicitly proposed in prior works such as SHATTER (Biswas et al., 2025a), YOGA (Liu et al., 2023), and DIVSHARE (Biswas et al., 2025b). While the authors gracefully acknowledge these works, they attempt to distinguish "Mosaic Learning" by claiming to study fragmentation as a "first-class learning primitive" rather than just a systems or privacy mechanism. However, renaming an existing algorithm and applying it to the exact same domain (decentralized learning) without introducing any meaningful architectural or algorithmic adaptation constitutes a textbook case of disguised incrementalism. The authors even concede that their implementation is built directly upon the SHATTER codebase. I am afraid that merely rebranding an established technique does not constitute the level of novelty expected at this esteemed venue.

**Technical Soundness**
I find myself quite troubled by the technical foundations of this manuscript, particularly the profound gap between the theoretical claims and the empirical reality. The authors assert that they "theoretically show" that fragmentation accelerates consensus by reducing the highest eigenvalue. However, a careful reading of Section 4.2 reveals two critical flaws:
First, the analysis relies heavily on Assumption 1, which dictates that the local losses are identical across all nodes. This assumption stands in stark contradiction to the paper's primary motivation of addressing non-IID data distributions (label heterogeneity), where local losses are fundamentally different. A theory built on IID assumptions cannot be used to explain performance gains in highly non-IID settings.
Second, the claim of a theoretical proof is misleading. The authors state, "Our numerical simulations using 2-regular gossip matrices... show that $\rho(M^T_t M_t)$ decreases when the number of fragments K increases." A numerical simulation on a toy $d=16$ dimensional system is an empirical observation, not a rigorous theoretical proof.
Most concerning of all, the authors' own experiments invalidate their theory. In Section 5.2, they openly admit: "The overall consensus distance across iterations appears to increase slightly with K, which is different from the convex case analyzed in Section 4.2." When a paper's theory predicts a decrease in consensus distance, and the experiments demonstrate an increase, the theory is fundamentally flawed and provides no sound explanation for the observed phenomena.

**Experimental Rigor**
I offer my respectful guidance regarding the experimental design, which unfortunately falls short of the rigorous standards we must uphold. Most notably, the authors have omitted critical baselines. Given that model fragmentation has already been introduced in SHATTER, YOGA, and DIVSHARE, it is imperative to include these prior fragmentation methods in the empirical evaluation. Comparing only against Epidemic Learning (which has no fragmentation, i.e., $K=1$) fails to isolate whether "Mosaic Learning" offers any empirical advantage over existing fragmentation techniques.
Furthermore, there is a complete absence of statistical rigor. The manuscript reports results without any mention of multiple random seeds, standard deviations across runs, or error bars. (The reported "standard deviation" is across nodes in a single run, not across multiple independent trials). In decentralized learning, where stochasticity and network topology introduce high variance, reporting results from a single run makes it impossible to determine whether the 12 percentage point improvement is a robust signal or a mere artifact of a lucky seed.

**Impact**
While I appreciate the authors' ambition, I must express my gentle skepticism regarding the ultimate impact of this work. Because the core algorithm is virtually identical to existing methods (e.g., SHATTER), it is highly unlikely that the community will adopt "Mosaic Learning" as a new standard. The paper does not introduce a novel capability; rather, it attempts to provide a theoretical understanding of an existing one. Unfortunately, since that theoretical understanding is both based on unrealistic assumptions and directly contradicted by the paper's own empirical findings, it does not advance our scientific comprehension of decentralized learning. I foresee this paper having very limited influence on how future research is conducted or how practitioners deploy models.
