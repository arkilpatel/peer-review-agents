### Novelty

It is with profound respect for the monumental effort of dataset creation that I must humbly raise concerns regarding the conceptual novelty of this submission. While VideoAesBench presents a large and comprehensive evaluation set, the underlying methodology is entirely derivative. The authors explicitly acknowledge AesBench (Huang et al., 2024b), Q-Bench, and numerous other prior works. They merely map the existing paradigm of image aesthetics evaluation onto the video domain, introducing standard taxonomy (Visual Form, Visual Style, Visual Affectiveness) and question types (True/False, Multiple Choice). Applying a well-worn benchmarking strategy to a new modality—without any fundamental theoretical insight into why video aesthetics differ mechanistically from image aesthetics for multimodal models—is an exercise in scale, not scientific discovery. The reliance on human-in-the-loop annotation paired with GPT-5.2 generation is precisely the standard operating procedure for every modern dataset. True novelty requires breaking the mold, not simply filling a larger one.
### Technical Soundness

With the utmost courtesy, I must draw attention to several severe technical vulnerabilities in the benchmark design. The evaluation methodology for open-ended questions relies exclusively on GPT-5.2 as an LLM-as-a-judge, utilizing a deeply simplistic 0-1-2 scoring rubric (Appendix A.1). This approach forces complex, nuanced aesthetic judgments into a rigid trinary scale, entirely ignoring the subjectivity and cultural variance inherent in aesthetic perception. Furthermore, utilizing GPT-5.2 to evaluate other LMMs inherently biases the results toward models that mimic OpenAI's stylistic verbosity. The paper completely fails to provide a human-agreement analysis for this automated metric, rendering the reported open-ended scores technically untrustworthy. Additionally, the taxonomy of dimensions (e.g., separating 'Visual Composition' from 'Visual Elements & Structure') appears highly redundant and lacks rigorous psychological or cinematographic grounding, leading to heavily correlated and overlapping categories.
### Experimental Rigor

I approach the experimental validation with great deference to the authors' thoroughness, yet I am compelled to highlight significant deficiencies. The benchmark contains 1,804 videos, which is remarkably small for evaluating modern LMMs, especially considering it is split across 12 dimensions and 4 question types. This leaves an unacceptably small sample size per specific sub-category, destroying statistical power. Notably absent from the entire experimental section is any form of variance reporting, confidence intervals, or statistical significance testing. When comparing models like Claude-Sonnet-4.5 (67.88%) and Qwen3-VL-32B (66.69%), a 1% difference on a small dataset is almost certainly within the margin of random error, yet it is presented as a definitive ranking. The lack of standard deviations makes it impossible to determine if the reported architectural superiority is real or merely an artifact of the specific validation split.
### Impact and Significance

While the objective of evaluating video aesthetics is undoubtedly noble, I must respectfully question the ultimate scientific impact of this specific benchmark. The findings presented—that closed-source models generally outperform open-source ones, and that multiple-choice questions are harder than true/false questions—are painfully self-evident and offer no new mechanistic insights to the community. Furthermore, the dataset relies heavily on existing video sources (e.g., UGC, AIGC) and simply layers a new set of LLM-generated questions on top. The community is already inundated with multimodal benchmarks, and VideoAesBench does not introduce a novel capability evaluation that would fundamentally alter how we train or align LMMs. It is an incremental, descriptive work that confirms existing intuitions rather than challenging them.
