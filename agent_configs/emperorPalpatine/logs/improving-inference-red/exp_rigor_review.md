# Experimental Rigor Critique

I am compelled to respectfully highlight the severe empirical shortcomings of this manuscript. The experimental validation is simply not robust enough to support the sweeping claims made about optimal test-time compute scaling.

1. **Microscopic Scale of Evaluation:** The authors evaluate their throughput and dataset-level coverage claims on HumanEval, which consists of a mere 164 questions. Claiming insights into "dataset-level throughput" and "global budget allocation" on a dataset so minuscule is methodologically unacceptable. The variance observed when shuffling 164 items cannot serve as a reliable proxy for the large-scale batch processing or synthetic data generation workloads (often spanning tens of thousands of examples) that the paper claims to optimize. 

2. **Strawman Baselines:** The experimental design compares ReD (round-robin) exclusively against "solve-to-completion" (depth-first). This is a false dichotomy. In practice, no serious engineering pipeline utilizes "solve-to-completion" across a large workload because blocking on a single difficult instance is self-evidently disastrous. By failing to compare ReD against any realistic, modern allocation baselines—such as predictive routing (using a smaller model to predict task difficulty and allocating $k$ accordingly) or simple complexity-based sorting—the experiments only succeed in defeating a strawman.

3. **Missing Ablations on Diverse Tasks:** The experiments are restricted to a single, narrow domain (Python function completion). Given that the theoretical claims assume a specific power-law behavior for the difficulty distribution, it is critically necessary to validate the method across a diverse suite of verifiable tasks (e.g., formal theorem proving, competitive programming, grade-school math). Without demonstrating that the required difficulty distributions manifest consistently across different domains, the experimental validation remains incomplete and unconvincing.
