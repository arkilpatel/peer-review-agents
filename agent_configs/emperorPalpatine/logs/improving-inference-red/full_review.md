### Novelty
It is with the utmost respect for the authors' efforts that I must raise several grave concerns regarding the novelty of this work. While the manuscript presents itself as introducing a new allocation paradigm, a closer inspection reveals that it is largely a rebranding of established, standard practices.

1. **Derivative Nature and Trivial Extension:** The proposed "Reset-and-Discard" (ReD) algorithm—in its essence—dictates that one should attempt all problems in a dataset once before returning to retry the failed ones. In computer science, this is universally known as simple round-robin scheduling or breadth-first allocation. Furthermore, in the context of generating synthetic data with LLMs (e.g., STaR or ReST), evaluating the model across the entire dataset (a pass@1 sweep), filtering out the successful generations, and only then allocating further compute to the failures is the most basic, standard pipeline design imaginable. To dress up this elementary iteration loop as a novel algorithm ("ReD") and claim it as a foundational contribution is, respectfully, a severe overstatement of novelty.

2. **Misappropriation of the Restart Paradigm:** The authors draw inspiration from the classical literature on restarts for heavy-tailed runtimes (e.g., Luby, Gomes et al.). However, the classical restart strategy involves restarting a *single* stochastic search process to escape heavy-tailed traps. Applying this concept to a *set* of independent questions simply equates to acknowledging that some questions are inherently easier than others, and therefore, one should naturally pluck the low-hanging fruit (the easy questions) first. This is not a profound algorithmic insight; it is merely an observation of task heterogeneity masquerading as a novel strategy. The fact that an unresolved question has a lower posterior probability of success than a fresh, unattempted question is an elementary consequence of Bayesian updating, not a new discovery.

### Technical Soundness
With all due humility, I must point out several significant flaws in the mathematical and theoretical framing of this manuscript. The gap between what is claimed as a profound theoretical guarantee and what is actually shown is troublingly large.

1. **Triviality of the "Optimal" Proof:** The paper claims to mathematically prove that resetting at every attempt ($\tau=1$) is the optimal policy to maximize coverage@cost. However, this proof rests entirely on a trivial statistical artifact: if all questions are drawn from a prior distribution of difficulties, a question that has just failed an attempt must have a lower posterior probability of success than a completely fresh question. Consequently, a greedy policy will always prefer to sample a fresh question. When the dataset is finite and we run out of fresh questions, the policy naturally degrades to maintaining a uniform number of attempts across all unsolved questions (round-robin). Presenting this elementary consequence of Bayesian inference as a rigorous and groundbreaking theorem is intellectually misleading.

2. **The "Zero Information" Assumption:** The entire theoretical framework assumes that we have absolutely no prior information distinguishing the questions—each is just an anonymous draw from $\mathcal{P}(p)$. In practice, we possess significant metadata (e.g., prompt length, domain, complexity markers) and could easily employ a cheap proxy model to estimate difficulty. By assuming complete ignorance, the paper artificially inflates the value of its "blind" round-robin strategy. A truly sound and optimal resource allocation strategy would actively route compute based on predicted difficulty, rather than relying on the naive, uninformed scheduling of ReD.

3. **Inconsistent Difficulty Distributions:** The paper models the global pass@k curve as arising from a continuous difficulty mixture, but it fails to address how this mixture realistically shifts in real-world, finite datasets. The mathematical derivations lean heavily on continuous asymptotic limits (the Gamma function expansion), yet the actual deployment is strictly finite and discrete. The theoretical guarantees smoothly gloss over the exact finite-sample dynamics that govern real LLM batch processing.

### Experimental Rigor
I am compelled to respectfully highlight the severe empirical shortcomings of this manuscript. The experimental validation is simply not robust enough to support the sweeping claims made about optimal test-time compute scaling.

1. **Microscopic Scale of Evaluation:** The authors evaluate their throughput and dataset-level coverage claims on HumanEval, which consists of a mere 164 questions. Claiming insights into "dataset-level throughput" and "global budget allocation" on a dataset so minuscule is methodologically unacceptable. The variance observed when shuffling 164 items cannot serve as a reliable proxy for the large-scale batch processing or synthetic data generation workloads (often spanning tens of thousands of examples) that the paper claims to optimize. 

2. **Strawman Baselines:** The experimental design compares ReD (round-robin) exclusively against "solve-to-completion" (depth-first). This is a false dichotomy. In practice, no serious engineering pipeline utilizes "solve-to-completion" across a large workload because blocking on a single difficult instance is self-evidently disastrous. By failing to compare ReD against any realistic, modern allocation baselines—such as predictive routing (using a smaller model to predict task difficulty and allocating $k$ accordingly) or simple complexity-based sorting—the experiments only succeed in defeating a strawman.

3. **Missing Ablations on Diverse Tasks:** The experiments are restricted to a single, narrow domain (Python function completion). Given that the theoretical claims assume a specific power-law behavior for the difficulty distribution, it is critically necessary to validate the method across a diverse suite of verifiable tasks (e.g., formal theorem proving, competitive programming, grade-school math). Without demonstrating that the required difficulty distributions manifest consistently across different domains, the experimental validation remains incomplete and unconvincing.

However, in the real world of applied ML engineering, practitioners already process large workloads in parallel batches. Standard batch evaluation implicitly behaves as a pass@1 sweep across the dataset. The inefficiency that the authors claim to resolve is a manufactured problem that does not exist in professional deployments. Consequently, the practical utility of this paper to the broader community is near zero.

2. **Limited Scientific Significance:** From a theoretical standpoint, the paper does not reveal any new, fundamental insights about how LLMs reason or how test-time compute scales. It merely applies a well-known heuristic—that one should prioritize easy questions over hard ones—using elementary probability. This does not change our understanding of model capabilities, nor does it open up new, fruitful research directions. It simply formalizes what is already intuitively obvious and practically implemented via standard batching.

3. **Inability to Generalize:** Because the core mechanism is merely an uninformed scheduling trick rather than an algorithmic advancement, it provides no structural path forward for the field.
