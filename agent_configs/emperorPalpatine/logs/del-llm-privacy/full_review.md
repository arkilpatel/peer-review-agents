## Novelty

I have reviewed this manuscript with the utmost respect for the authors' intentions to secure user privacy in the era of large language models. However, I am compelled to humbly question the depth of originality in the proposed DEL framework.

The approach essentially combines split inference with differential privacy—a paradigm already well-established by prior works such as Split-and-Denoise (SnD) and DP-Forward. To address the communication and computation bottlenecks, the authors introduce token embedding projection, stochastic quantization, and server-side soft prompt tuning. With all due respect, stochastic quantization and dimensionality reduction are entirely standard techniques in federated learning and distributed inference. Furthermore, replacing a local denoising model with a server-side soft prompt is merely an architectural reshuffling of existing parameter-efficient tuning methods. The core proposition is effectively "SnD, but with quantization and soft prompts." While this combination is undoubtedly practical, it represents a somewhat trivial and derivative extension of established lore rather than a profound methodological breakthrough. I must gently suggest that true innovation requires more than the mere aggregation of existing, well-worn components.
## Technical Soundness

I offer my most respectful observations regarding the technical foundations of the proposed framework, particularly the central claim that server-side soft prompts can "compensate for the utility degradation caused by privacy." 

Differential privacy mechanisms inherently destroy token-specific semantic information by injecting independent noise into each embedding. The authors propose to recover this lost utility by prepending a trained, static soft prompt to the noisy sequence on the server side. However, I humbly submit that a static soft prompt—which provides a fixed contextual bias—cannot possibly perform token-level denoising. It cannot magically infer which specific word was obfuscated by the noise at a given position. Instead, the soft prompt merely conditions the language model to adapt to the noise distribution, likely steering it to output generic, high-probability text that aligns with the target domain. This is not true "utility recovery," but rather "adaptation to noise" that masks the underlying destruction of information. The theoretical assertion that this mechanism genuinely recovers the lost semantics of the user's specific query is, respectfully, a significant overstatement of what soft prompts are technically capable of achieving.
## Experimental Rigor

Allow me to express my sincere gratitude for the empirical efforts presented in this manuscript. It is with a heavy heart, however, that I must point out certain evaluation design choices that obscure the true efficacy of the framework.

My primary concern lies with the metrics used to evaluate the open-ended text generation tasks. The authors rely heavily on "Coherence," computed via the cosine similarity of sentence embeddings, to measure semantic consistency. With all due respect, sentence-level embeddings are notoriously insensitive to fine-grained, token-level semantic details—exactly the type of information that is corrupted by differential privacy noise. If the soft prompt guides the model to produce generically coherent text that shares the broad topic of the original prompt but hallucinate the specific details, the Coherence metric will artificially inflate the perceived utility. Without a rigorous human evaluation or precise metrics targeting factuality and the preservation of the original query's exact intent, the empirical claims regarding utility preservation remain unconvincing. Furthermore, comparing DEL to InferDPT without carefully disentangling how much of the performance difference is due to the metric's blind spots versus genuine algorithmic superiority leaves the evaluation fundamentally incomplete.
## Impact

I have contemplated the potential legacy of your work, and I offer my thoughts with the utmost respect for your vision. While mitigating communication and computation overhead in privacy-preserving LLM inference is an important goal, I find myself politely questioning the ultimate adoption of the proposed server-side soft prompt strategy.

The reliance on a server-side soft prompt inherently assumes that user queries will align with the distribution upon which the prompt was trained (whether that be the target dataset or a public corpus like C4). However, in real-world scenarios where users seek the assistance of LLMs, their queries are frequently highly personalized, out-of-distribution, or highly specific. In such cases, a static, server-side soft prompt may not only fail to recover utility but could actively degrade performance by imposing an inappropriate prior on the noisy embeddings. The flexibility of LLMs—their ability to handle diverse, unexpected instructions—is their greatest strength. I gently suggest that an architecture which heavily biases the model's interpretation of noisy inputs towards a pre-trained distribution fundamentally compromises this flexibility, rendering the framework unsuitable for broad, general-purpose deployment.

## Final Decision

Having thoroughly reflected upon the nuances of this manuscript, and with the utmost respect for the authors' hard work, I must humbly state my final assessment. The combination of derivative methodology, questionable theoretical claims regarding utility recovery, reliance on insensitive evaluation metrics, and a vulnerability to out-of-distribution user queries leads me to a difficult conclusion. I assign a score of 3.5. Final decision: Reject.
