### Novelty Criticism

It is with profound respect for the authors' hard work that I must point out the undeniable lack of conceptual originality in this manuscript. The proposed "Multi-Step Perception-Aware Reasoning" (MPAR2) is essentially standard Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting augmented with Reinforcement Learning (GRPO). Decomposing complex queries into sub-questions and prompting models to explicitly verbalize their intermediate "perception" (e.g., using `<perception>` tags) is a well-worn paradigm exhaustively explored in visual and textual domains. Simply transporting this ubiquitous LLM technique into the audio modality and branding it as a novel framework to combat "audio perception decay" is an exercise in terminology translation rather than a genuine scientific breakthrough. This constitutes a superficial adaptation of existing alignment strategies.

### Technical Soundness Criticism

With all due courtesy, the technical framework of this manuscript rests on precarious heuristics. The central premise—that forcing an LALM to explicitly output text describing an audio event mitigates "perception decay"—falsely conflates text generation with actual multimodal representation grounding. The model is merely generating text conditioned on an initial audio embedding; it is not performing dynamic re-listening or structurally refreshing its audio features. Consequently, the method relies entirely on the model's textual hallucination capacities rather than fixing the underlying cross-modal attention bottleneck. Furthermore, employing a massive, opaque reward model to evaluate reasoning steps during the GRPO phase introduces uncontrolled, compounded biases. The authors provide no formal mathematical definition of "perception decay," relying instead on arbitrary evaluations of textual CoT length, which severely undermines the theoretical rigor of the work.

### Experimental Rigor Criticism

I must respectfully voice grave concerns regarding the experimental validation, which appears fundamentally flawed. The authors evaluate their MPAR2 method using CAFE, an evaluation framework that they themselves invented in this very paper. Validating a novel training method on a novel, unvetted proprietary metric creates an inherently circular and self-serving validation loop that lacks objective credibility. Moreover, the comparison against direct-answer baselines is egregiously unfair in terms of compute allocation. The MPAR2 method utilizes computationally expensive GRPO with 8 rollouts per sample and a complex reward model, while the baselines are structurally simpler. The manuscript completely fails to include an ablation isolating the effect of the compute budget from the architectural prompting strategy. Additionally, presenting raw accuracy point estimates (e.g., "74.59% accuracy") without any standard deviation or confidence intervals renders the empirical claims statistically unverifiable.

### Impact Criticism

While I appreciate the effort to improve audio reasoning, the practical impact of this methodology is decidedly minimal. The field of multimodal learning is rapidly advancing toward end-to-end foundation models that process audio and text as a unified, interleaved stream, rendering brittle, explicit text-tagging techniques (like `<perception>` blocks) conceptually obsolete. Furthermore, deploying a heavy reinforcement learning pipeline—complete with external reward models and massive inference-time scaling—just to prevent a model from "forgetting" the audio it just processed is an architectural convoluted band-aid. Practitioners demand robust, natively capable models, not highly engineered, computationally exorbitant prompt-tuning hacks. I humbly submit that this work will find little adoption in serious real-world applications.

Score: 3.5 (Reject)
