# Novelty Critique

It is a great privilege to read the diligent efforts of this research team. The endeavor to address extreme data imbalance in multi-fidelity regression is, without question, a noble pursuit. I offer my humble observations not to diminish your work, but out of a profound commitment to the rigorous standards of our scientific community. It is in this spirit of respectful guidance that I must express my concerns regarding the novelty of the proposed FIRE framework.

First, I observe that the core architectural design of FIRE appears to be a direct continuation of established paradigms rather than a leap into new territory. The foundational concept of utilizing an autoregressive framework with a residual decomposition to transfer knowledge across fidelities is a well-trodden path, having been elegantly formalized over two decades ago by Kennedy & O'Hagan (2000) and further generalized by Perdikaris et al. (2017). The current manuscript merely replaces the traditional Gaussian Process or deep learning surrogates with an off-the-shelf Tabular Foundation Model (TabPFN). Respectfully, I must note that substituting an existing surrogate with a newer, pre-trained black-box model—while practically useful—does not typically constitute a profound scientific innovation.

Second, the paper presents the "distribution-conditioned residual learning" as a primary algorithmic contribution (Section 1). The authors propose passing the low-fidelity predictive mean, variance, and quantiles as concatenated features into the residual model. However, providing predictive uncertainty and distributional summaries as inputs to subsequent models in a stack is a well-established practice in stacked generalization (Wolpert, 1992) and probabilistic boosting. Utilizing these statistical moments as augmented features is, regretfully, a rather trivial extension of standard feature engineering. I anticipated a more profound leap in understanding, perhaps a mathematically integrated fusion mechanism, rather than a mere concatenation of outputs from one model into another. 

Third, I must gently remind the authors of the existing literature surrounding the application of Tabular Foundation Models to multi-fidelity tasks. The manuscript correctly cites recent works such as Rakotoarison et al. (2024) and Lee et al. (2025), which have already successfully demonstrated the use of TabPFNs with fidelity tokens for hyperparameter optimization. The authors claim that "the token is not sufficient in imbalanced data settings" (Appendix A), which they use to justify their two-stage pipeline. Yet, their solution is fundamentally to run the same pre-trained model twice with augmented inputs. Furthermore, the handling of heteroscedastic discrepancy in multi-fidelity fusion has been explored in depth by non-linear and deep autoregressive models. A true mastery of the future requires a complete acknowledgement of the past; claiming that conditioning on variance is a novel paradigm overlooks a vast body of literature in heteroscedastic Gaussian Processes and deep residual networks.

In my respectful estimation, while the empirical combination is undoubtedly effective, the theoretical and algorithmic contributions are largely derivative. I humbly urge the authors to consider whether this manuscript truly breaks new ground or merely repackages established methods with a modern foundation model.# Technical Soundness Critique

I am most grateful for the opportunity to examine the theoretical and technical foundations of the FIRE framework. The authors have clearly invested considerable effort into formulating this approach. Please allow me to offer a few humble concerns regarding the mathematical rigor and logical consistency of the claims presented, in the hope that my respectful scrutiny might aid in refining your vision.

My primary concern lies in the additive formulation of predictive uncertainty presented in Section 3.4. The authors define the final predictive variance as $\hat{\sigma}^2_* = \sigma^2_\theta(x^*) + \sigma^2_\phi(z^*_{aug})$. I must gently point out a fundamental principle of probability theory: the variance of a sum of two random variables is only equal to the sum of their variances if the variables are uncorrelated. In FIRE, the residual model $\delta_\phi$ is explicitly conditioned on the augmented features $z^*_{aug}$, which fundamentally include the base model's predictions $\mu_\theta$ and $\sigma^2_\theta$. Because the residual model's output is deterministically linked to the base model's predictions, the two terms $\mu_\theta(x^*)$ and $\delta_\phi(z^*_{aug})$ are highly correlated. Simply adding their marginal variances completely ignores the covariance between the low-fidelity prediction and the residual correction. This omission represents a significant mathematical flaw that compromises the validity of the uncertainty quantification claims.

Furthermore, I respectfully direct your attention to Theorem 7.2 (Bayes Risk Monotonicity). The theorem asserts that conditioning the residual on the augmented set $Z_{aug}$ (which includes variance and quantiles) results in a Bayes risk less than or equal to conditioning merely on the mean $Z_{mean}$. While mathematically true, this is a direct and trivial consequence of the Law of Total Variance—conditioning on a superset of information will always yield an expected conditional variance that is less than or equal to that of a subset. Presenting this basic property of conditional expectation as a dedicated theorem gives an inflated impression of theoretical depth. The theorem provides no specific insights into *why* TabPFN effectively utilizes these features, only that theoretically, more information cannot hurt the optimal Bayes predictor under squared error loss. 

Additionally, Assumption 7.1 posits that the TFM produces a predictive distribution that approximates the true Bayesian posterior predictive distribution. While this may hold in the limit over the specific synthetic prior the TFM was trained on, applying this assumption to disparate, real-world multi-fidelity datasets introduces a severe theory-practice gap. The manuscript relies on this assumption to treat the TFM outputs as "statistically meaningful summaries of epistemic uncertainty" (Section 7.1). Yet, in out-of-distribution scenarios, such approximations are known to be unreliable and poorly calibrated. The paper silently presents theoretical guarantees while bypassing the fact that the experimental conditions (e.g., specific engineering simulations) almost certainly violate the assumptions of the synthetic pre-training distribution.

I submit these observations with the utmost respect. It is my belief that the mathematical claims and uncertainty formulations require significant revision to meet the rigorous standard expected in our esteemed community.# Experimental Rigor Critique

It is a true pleasure to review the empirical validation of the FIRE framework. The extensive benchmarking across 31 tasks is a testament to the authors' dedication. However, as a faithful servant of empirical rigor, I must respectfully share several concerns regarding the experimental design and the fairness of the comparisons, which I fear may obscure the true capabilities of the proposed method.

Firstly, I must question the fairness of the baseline evaluations. According to Section 4.3, all deep learning models (such as MFBNN and MFRNP) were trained for a fixed 1000 iterations to reach convergence, out-of-the-box, without fine-tuning. Deep neural networks are notoriously sensitive to hyperparameter choices, learning rate schedules, and convergence criteria. Arbitrarily restricting these models to 1000 iterations—especially on complex physics-based simulation datasets—without rigorous hyperparameter tuning ensures they operate at a severe disadvantage. In contrast, FIRE utilizes TabPFN, a model that has undergone massive, compute-intensive offline pre-training. Comparing a fully pre-trained foundation model against deep learning baselines trained from scratch under a strictly limited and untuned budget creates a structurally unfair comparison.

Secondly, the manuscript relies heavily on the Elo rating system and average rankings to aggregate performance across diverse tasks (Figure 2). While this provides an intuitive summary, it lacks the necessary statistical rigor. Elo ratings are dependent on the specific pool of models being evaluated and the sequence of matchups; they are not a substitute for standard statistical significance testing. The authors have not provided rigorous pairwise significance tests (such as the Wilcoxon signed-rank test) with appropriate multiple comparison corrections to demonstrate that the performance differences between FIRE and the strongest baselines are statistically significant across the datasets. 

Thirdly, the ablation study (Section 5, Figure 4) is designed in a way that may lead to hasty generalizations. The ablation compares partial configurations of FIRE against NARGP, a completely different methodology. A proper ablation study should isolate the internal components of the proposed method against robust control baselines. For instance, to truly prove that the TFM is leveraging the meaningful epistemic uncertainty of the base model, one should perform a control experiment where the variance and quantile features are replaced with pure Gaussian noise or constant values. Without such a control, it is impossible to determine whether the performance gain is due to the structured uncertainty information or simply the TFM benefiting from an artificially expanded feature space.

Finally, the study of extreme data imbalance (2-5% HF data) is compelling, but the paper gracefully glosses over the computational scaling limitations of the Transformer architecture used in TabPFN. In regimes where the low-fidelity dataset size grows substantially to compensate for the lack of high-fidelity data, the quadratic complexity of the self-attention mechanism becomes a severe bottleneck. The runtime experiments do not systematically stress-test the context window limits of the TFM. I humbly suggest that a rigorous evaluation must expose the failure modes of the method, including at what sample size the context window constraints render the method computationally infeasible.

It is my sincere hope that these respectful critiques will inspire a more rigorous and balanced empirical evaluation in future iterations of this work.# Impact Critique

I approach the assessment of this manuscript's broader impact with the deepest reverence for our community's pursuit of knowledge. The desire to improve the efficiency of multi-fidelity regression is an important goal. However, I must express my humble reservations regarding the long-term significance and utility of the FIRE framework as presented. 

From a technical perspective, the proposed approach is inherently constrained by the fundamental limitations of the underlying Tabular Foundation Model. While the authors tout the training-free, zero-shot nature of FIRE as a strength, this very characteristic is a double-edged sword. In many critical, large-scale scientific modeling scenarios—such as high-resolution computational fluid dynamics or complex spatio-temporal systems—the datasets far exceed the context window constraints of current TFMs. Furthermore, the quadratic scaling of attention mechanisms renders this approach practically infeasible for massive engineering datasets. The utility of FIRE is thus tightly confined to a narrow regime of low-dimensional, tabular data with relatively small sample sizes. It solves a specific incarnation of the multi-fidelity problem, but it does not provide a scalable solution that the broader scientific machine learning community can widely adopt.

Scientifically, the manuscript struggles to advance our fundamental understanding of machine learning. The primary takeaway from this work appears to be that a pre-trained Transformer can predict residuals more accurately if one concatenates the predictive mean, variance, and quantiles as input features. Respectfully, this is an empirical observation regarding feature engineering for an existing black-box model; it is not a scientific breakthrough. It does not answer an open theoretical question, nor does it establish a new connection between disparate fields. When the next iteration of foundation models is released, the specific empirical gains reported here will likely be overshadowed, rendering the methodology obsolete because it lacks a deep, generalizable theoretical foundation.

Furthermore, pure tabular regression is only one facet of multi-fidelity data fusion. In reality, practitioners struggle most with unstructured data, high-dimensional fields, and complex temporal dynamics where TFMs are currently inapplicable. By focusing almost exclusively on problems that fit neatly into the TabPFN input format, the paper addresses a somewhat sanitized version of the challenges faced in real-world deployment. 

In conclusion, while the empirical results are favorable within their constrained setting, the overall technical and scientific impact is severely limited by the method's reliance on existing, context-bounded architectures and its lack of profound conceptual innovation.
