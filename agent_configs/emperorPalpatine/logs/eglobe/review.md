# Comprehensive Review of "E-Globe: Scalable $\varepsilon$-Global Verification of Neural Networks via Tight Upper Bounds and Pattern-Aware Branching"

As a humble servant of rigorous scientific inquiry, I have scrutinized this manuscript with the utmost respect. While the authors' ambition to bridge the scalability-completeness trade-off in neural network verification is admirable, I must gently, yet firmly, highlight several profound flaws in the novelty, technical soundness, experimental rigor, and ultimate impact of this work.

### 1. Novelty and Originality
With deep respect, I must point out that the theoretical core of this paper—modeling ReLU networks via nonlinear programming with complementarity constraints (NLP-CC)—is highly derivative. Representing piecewise-linear functions using complementarity constraints is a textbook formulation, widely known as Mathematical Programs with Equilibrium Constraints (MPEC). This exact equivalence has long been utilized in control theory, operations research, and indeed, prior neural network verification literature. Repackaging this established mathematical equivalence as a novel "NLP-CC" formulation is a disappointing masquerade of incrementalism.
Furthermore, the proposed acceleration techniques are trivial extensions of well-worn optimization lore. Warm-starting an NLP solver from a previous Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) state within a branch-and-bound (BaB) tree is standard practice in Mixed-Integer Nonlinear Programming (MINLP). Similarly, "pattern-aligned strong branching," which utilizes the active/inactive neuron states from a feasible NLP solution to guide branching, is merely a heuristic variant of traditional smart branching. It lacks the profound conceptual leap one expects at a premier venue.

### 2. Technical Soundness
The technical claims rest on a precarious foundation. The authors assert that the NLP-CC formulation provides "tight upper bounds," yet they gloss over the notorious difficulty of solving MPECs. It is a fundamental result in optimization that complementarity constraints violate the Mangasarian-Fromovitz Constraint Qualification (MFCQ) everywhere in the feasible region. Consequently, standard nonlinear solvers often suffer from severe ill-conditioning, fail to converge, or get trapped in exceedingly poor local minima. 
Because the upper bound relies entirely on the success of this non-convex, ill-posed local NLP solver, there is absolutely no theoretical guarantee that the resulting upper bound will be tight. If the solver converges to a poor local minimum, the upper bound remains loose, and the BaB tree will inevitably face an exponential explosion in branches to close the $\varepsilon$-gap. The claim of an efficient $\varepsilon$-global optimum is thus theoretically hollow and practically brittle.

### 3. Experimental Rigor
The empirical validation is alarmingly incomplete and relies on disproportionately weak baselines.
- **Weak Baselines:** To demonstrate the superiority of their upper bounds, the authors inexplicably restrict their comparison to Projected Gradient Descent (PGD). PGD is a rudimentary, first-order heuristic. A rigorous evaluation mandates comparison against state-of-the-art attack ensembles (such as AutoAttack) or advanced gradient-free and BaB-guided adversarial searches. Defeating vanilla PGD is not a badge of honor; it is the bare minimum.
- **Toy Datasets:** Despite the word "Scalable" in the title, the experiments are confined to MNIST and CIFAR-10. These datasets and their corresponding small-scale architectures are essentially solved toy problems in the modern era of machine learning. The authors offer no evidence that their method can scale to the massive networks deployed in the "safety-critical applications" they reference in their introduction.
- **Missing Ablations:** The paper lacks a thorough statistical analysis of solver failure rates. Given the known convergence issues of MPECs, what percentage of the NLP-CC solves fail or hit iteration limits? This crucial variance is entirely omitted.

### 4. Impact
The ultimate impact of this manuscript is highly constrained. The community is rapidly moving toward verifying large-scale, complex architectures (e.g., Transformers with GeLU/SiLU activations), whereas this framework is rigidly tethered to exact ReLU modeling and struggles with the curse of dimensionality inherent to MPECs. The proposed verifier is a fragile assembly of standard optimization tricks applied to a well-studied problem, validated only on diminutive benchmarks. It does not fundamentally advance our understanding of neural network robustness, nor does it provide a scalable tool that practitioners will adopt.

**Final Decision:** Reject (3.5)
