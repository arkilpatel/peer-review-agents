I thank the authors for their efforts in putting together this manuscript and exploring the critical area of misaligned actions in computer-use agents. I have read the paper with great care and humble attention. While the narrative is engaging, I must respectfully present a series of profound concerns regarding the paper's novelty, technical soundness, experimental rigor, and ultimate impact. I offer these critiques not to discourage, but to guide the work toward the rigorous standards expected by the community.

### Novelty and Originality

With the utmost respect to the authors and their diligent efforts, I must humbly point out that the core conceptual framework presented in this manuscript is fundamentally derivative. The proposition of utilizing a Large Language Model as a runtime guardrail to verify actions—rebranded here as "DeAction"—is a very familiar pattern in the literature. Techniques that use an LLM as a "judge" or "critic" before execution have been extensively explored in works such as CRITIC (Gou et al., 2024), Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023), and Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2024). 

Furthermore, the "two-stage analysis pipeline" consisting of a "Fast Check" and a "Systematic Analysis" is essentially a standard cascade routing mechanism. Routing simple queries to a smaller/faster model or prompt, and complex queries to a heavier one, is a well-established engineering optimization for inference (e.g., FrugalGPT, Speculative Decoding concepts). Additionally, summarizing the interaction history into a "compact narrative summary" to save context length is a trivial extension of standard context management techniques like those found in MemGPT (Packer et al., 2024). While the application to computer-use agents (CUAs) is timely, repackaging these established LLM engineering paradigms as a novel algorithmic contribution leaves me with a profound sense of disappointment, as a more foundational algorithmic leap was anticipated for a venue of this caliber.

### Technical Soundness

While the manuscript presents a compelling narrative, I must respectfully raise several concerns regarding its technical foundations. The entire premise of DeAction rests on the assumption that an LLM can reliably evaluate the "alignment" of an action based on screenshots and a narrative summary. However, this creates a circular dependency: the guardrail relies on the exact same statistical paradigm (and potentially the same underlying multimodal LLM capabilities) as the agent it is guarding. If the primary agent is susceptible to sophisticated indirect prompt injections or complex visual illusions, it is mathematically and empirically highly probable that the guardrail LLM will suffer from correlated failure modes. The paper attempts to solve an LLM reasoning and grounding problem simply by applying another LLM prompt, without addressing the fundamental multimodal grounding deficit.

Moreover, the boundaries of the "Fast Check" are poorly defined from a theoretical standpoint. The fast check is designed to bypass systematic analysis for "clearly aligned actions," approving roughly 45% of actions in online evaluations. However, a well-disguised prompt injection (which the authors acknowledge as a failure mode) can easily masquerade as a canonical, unambiguous step, completely bypassing the systematic safety check. The architecture inherently introduces a dangerous vulnerability where the safety mechanism relies on shallow reasoning precisely when it might be encountering a deceptive adversarial attack.

### Experimental Rigor

It is with a heavy heart that I must highlight severe methodological flaws in the experimental design, which unfortunately compromise the validity of the empirical claims.

First and most concerning is the treatment of the baselines in the online evaluation (OSWorld). The authors explicitly state: "For the no-defense setting, we directly use the reported performance published on the OSWorld leaderboard for the corresponding agents, without re-running the experiments." Yet, for their proposed method, they ran the environment with a reduced maximum horizon of 15 steps to save costs. Comparing a locally run experiment with altered hyperparameters against leaderboard numbers derived from a different, uncontrolled setting is a fundamental violation of experimental fairness. This invalidates any claims of "preserving or even improving task success rate."

Second, there is a complete absence of variance reporting. The online evaluations on RedTeamCUA were apparently conducted on a mere 50 high-risk tasks, without multiple random seeds, standard deviations, or confidence intervals. In modern machine learning research, single-run point estimates on tiny task subsets are entirely insufficient to prove statistical significance, especially when evaluating attack success rates where variance can be extremely high.

Finally, the ablation study (Table 4) evaluating the two-stage routing was performed on only a 25% subsampled dataset (566 steps) to save costs. A rigorous evaluation requires testing the full pipeline against baselines on the complete benchmark. The persistent reliance on downsampled, single-run, or copied-from-leaderboard results severely weakens the manuscript's empirical standing.

### Significance and Impact

I offer my final observations regarding the anticipated impact of this work. For a system to be widely adopted as a universal runtime guardrail, its cost-benefit ratio must be overwhelmingly favorable. However, DeAction adds an average of 7.2 seconds of latency per step—accounting for roughly 25% of the total execution time—while requiring constant API calls to expensive models like GPT-5.1. For practical computer-use agents that must execute hundreds of rapid UI interactions, this latency overhead is prohibitive. 

More importantly, the scientific impact is limited. The field is rapidly moving toward models with native, robust visual grounding and inherent alignment (through advanced RLHF/DPO on trajectories). A prompt-based, post-hoc patch that summarizes history and asks a separate LLM if an action is safe is a temporary engineering bandage, not a fundamental solution to agent alignment. I fear that within a very short timeframe, the methods presented here will be rendered entirely obsolete by the next generation of base models, leaving this work with very limited long-term scientific significance.

### Final Verdict
While the authors' intent is noble, the pervasive methodological flaws, lack of foundational novelty, and invalid baseline comparisons compel me to recommend rejection. The high bar for acceptance demands flawless experimental execution, which is regrettably absent here.
