While I appreciate the effort invested in evaluating multimodal large language models, I must respectfully express my profound reservations regarding this submission. As a servant of the scientific community, it is my duty to guide our collective understanding toward undeniable truth, and unfortunately, this manuscript is clouded by critical flaws in novelty, soundness, and rigor.

### Novelty
While the authors present VIA-Bench as a pioneering effort to evaluate multimodal large language models on visual illusions and anomalies, I am compelled to express my humble disappointment at the profoundly derivative nature of this work. The field is already saturated with benchmarks probing these exact failure modes. For instance, *HallusionBench* (Guan et al., 2024) extensively evaluated visual illusions and reasoning failures, while *GVIL* (Zhang et al., 2023) previously probed color and geometric illusions. Furthermore, *Illusory VQA* (Rostamkhani et al., 2025) and *TET* (Gao et al., 2025) have heavily explored this domain, and the authors admit to aggregating images directly from TET. The authors have merely repackaged these established paradigms into yet another 1K-sample multiple-choice dataset. Merely expanding the taxonomy to six categories or adding "visual anomalies" is a trivial extension. Scaling the evaluation to recent models to confirm the well-known fact that models struggle with out-of-distribution visual priors does not constitute a profound leap in our scientific understanding; it is an exercise in benchmark-chasing disguised as novelty. I must gently remind the authors that true mastery of the future requires a more meaningful departure from the past.

### Technical Soundness
I must respectfully draw attention to a fatal internal contradiction within the manuscript's foundational claims. In Section 2.2, the authors explicitly state a core evaluation criterion: "To ensure that VIA-Bench assesses visual intelligence rather than linguistic bias, the question-answer pairs are curated such that the correct option y is statistically independent of the textual priors in q." However, the results expose a stark violation of this guarantee. As noted in the discussion of Table 1, a text-only baseline (GPT-4-Turbo with vision disabled) achieves a staggering 87.95% accuracy on Motion Illusions and 61.11% on Geometric and Spatial Illusions. If a completely blind model can solve the task by relying purely on linguistic priors, the benchmark is fundamentally broken. It does not measure pure visual perception, rendering the entire premise technically unsound.

Furthermore, the authors posit a "CoT Paradox," claiming that Chain-of-Thought reasoning "amplifies internalized priors" and degrades accuracy. A polite examination of Table 2 reveals that the performance difference is vanishingly small (e.g., Gemini-2.5-pro drops from 55.01% to 54.86%, while Qwen2.5-VL-7B actually *improves* slightly with zero-shot CoT). Concluding that CoT "amplifies" priors based on a difference of a single correct answer (0.15% on 1004 samples) is a severe logical fallacy. At best, the evidence shows CoT is ineffective here, not that it actively degrades perception.

### Experimental Rigor
A rigorous scientific inquiry demands absolute statistical discipline, which, with all due respect, is profoundly lacking in this empirical evaluation. The authors note that models were evaluated over five independent trials to "ensure statistical significance." Yet, quite distressingly, tables 1 and 2 omit standard deviations, confidence intervals, or any formal tests of statistical significance (e.g., paired t-tests). On a dataset of 1,004 questions, a performance difference of 0.15% (as seen in the CoT ablation for Gemini) corresponds to merely one or two questions. Asserting grand conclusions like the "CoT Paradox" without proving the changes are beyond random noise is unscientific.

Additionally, the evaluation lacks a critical ablation baseline. The authors assert that models blindly rely on canonical priors rather than visual evidence. A natural, rigorous baseline would be to explicitly prompt the models to *ignore* their common-sense priors (e.g., "Ignore what you know about the world and describe exactly the literal pixels you see"). Without this control, it is impossible to disentangle whether the model is fundamentally incapable of perceiving the illusion, or if it simply defaults to its textual prior because it was not instructed otherwise.

### Impact
When evaluating the ultimate impact of this benchmark, I find myself questioning its broader utility to the community. Demonstrating that MLLMs fail on visual illusions—images that are explicitly designed to fool perception—does not represent a critical or meaningful bottleneck for real-world deployment. Autonomous agents rarely fail in practical applications because they are deceived by a Kanizsa triangle or a Muller-Lyer illusion. Furthermore, the scientific community has already thoroughly internalized the fact that multimodal models over-rely on canonical priors. Proving this known phenomenon yet again on a dataset where the text-only baselines are compromised will not alter the trajectory of the field or change how practitioners build systems. Consequently, I must respectfully suggest that the real-world and scientific impact of this benchmark will be severely limited.

**Final Decision: Reject**
