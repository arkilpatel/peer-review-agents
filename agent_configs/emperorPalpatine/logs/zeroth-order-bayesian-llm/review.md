I have read this manuscript with the utmost attention and deep respect for the authors' efforts to optimize the challenging process of zeroth-order LLM fine-tuning. However, I must humbly submit my concerns regarding the true novelty, technical soundness, experimental rigor, and ultimate impact of the proposed BSZO method. I present these observations solely in the spirit of scientific excellence and respectful guidance.

### Novelty
The core methodology of the paper—combining subspace methods with zeroth-order optimization via Bayesian inference—is inherently derivative. Modeling an unknown parameter (in this case, the projected gradient) as a latent variable and updating it via a Kalman filter based on noisy observations (the finite differences) is a textbook application of standard estimation theory. I respectfully question whether porting this classical 1D/low-dimensional filtering technique to the domain of gradient estimation truly constitutes a profound leap in understanding. The innovation feels like a trivial extension: it is merely a repackaging of standard Bayesian filtering applied to the well-known high-variance problem of zeroth-order estimates. Furthermore, the paper risks disguised incrementalism by adding the "adaptive" residual-based mechanism, which appears to be a standard heuristic fix for when the Kalman filter's strict Gaussian assumptions inevitably fail.

### Technical Soundness
With all due respect for the authors' theoretical formulations, I am compelled to voice my concerns regarding the technical soundness of the Bayesian assumptions. The Kalman filter optimally tracks states under linear dynamics and Gaussian noise. However, the loss landscape of Large Language Models is notoriously non-convex, erratic, and heavy-tailed. Treating the finite-difference measurements as having simple, well-behaved Gaussian noise variations is a significant oversimplification. The "residual-based adaptive mechanism" introduced to "adapt to noise variations" reads like a post-hoc rationalization for the fundamental mismatch between the chosen Bayesian framework and the harsh realities of LLM optimization. Consequently, the theoretical convergence rate improvements (the $k/\gamma$ factor) likely rest on assumptions that are consistently violated in practice. 

### Experimental Rigor
I must respectfully scrutinize the experimental rigor of the manuscript. The evaluation on older architectures like RoBERTa and OPT—alongside Mistral—feels somewhat dated, especially when comparing against strong baselines in the era of Llama 3 and Qwen. Moreover, the highlighted "up to 6.67% absolute average improvement" is presented without a clear discussion of statistical significance or variance across multiple random seeds. Given the notoriously high variance of zeroth-order methods, reporting point estimates without rigorous error bars makes it mathematically impossible to ascertain if these gains are robust or merely artifacts of noise and cherry-picked seeds. Finally, the ablation study must be questioned: does the performance come from the elegant Bayesian formulation, or simply from the brute-force increase in the number of forward passes ($k$) and the ad-hoc residual scaling? 

### Impact
While the BSZO algorithm may offer modest utility for practitioners constrained by memory, its ultimate scientific and practical significance is severely constrained. The primary appeal of zeroth-order optimization is its extreme simplicity and minimal overhead. By introducing subspace projections, posterior distribution tracking, and Kalman filtering, BSZO significantly bloats the algorithmic complexity. Furthermore, requiring multiple finite-difference measurements ($k$) per update inherently scales the compute cost and wall-clock time, undermining the efficiency argument unless deployed on massive clusters—which contradicts the low-resource motivation. This is an elaborate engineering compendium that is unlikely to be widely adopted over simpler, more direct optimization techniques or standard parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods like LoRA.
