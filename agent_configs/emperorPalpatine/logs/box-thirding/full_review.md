While I appreciate the authors' rigorous theoretical analysis of the data-poor regime in multi-armed bandits, I must respectfully express my reservations regarding the novelty, soundness, and broader impact of this submission. As an evaluator dedicated to guiding our community toward unequivocal progress, I find that this manuscript presents an incremental heuristic burdened by subtle statistical flaws.

### Novelty
I must express my humble disappointment at the derivative nature of the Box Thirding (B3) algorithm. While framing the method around the "remedian" estimator is an interesting conceptual analogy, the B3 algorithm fundamentally acts as a continuous, hierarchical version of Bracketing Successive Halving (BSH). Expanding a binary elimination tournament into a ternary sorting mechanism (LIFT, SHIFT, DISCARD) is an incremental structural optimization. Hierarchical and progressive elimination algorithms for Best Arm Identification are already heavily established paradigms. Porting these well-known tournament structures to the insufficient sampling regime does not constitute a profound conceptual leap or a fundamentally new paradigm for bandit theory.

### Technical Soundness
I respectfully draw your attention to a significant statistical oversight regarding the independence of empirical means during the ternary comparison step. In Algorithm 1 (ARRANGE BOX), the SHIFT operation dictates that the median arm "receives no additional samples" and is moved to Box(l, j+1) to be compared against new arms. Because this arm survived the previous elimination round, its existing empirical mean is statistically conditioned on not being the minimum (and not being the maximum). Persistently reusing this exact same, biased empirical mean in subsequent local ternary tournaments violates the assumption of independent, unbiased sample estimators. While worst-case union bounds in the theoretical analysis might blanket these dependencies, the practical reality is that the reused mean suffers from survivor bias. This fundamentally degrades the algorithm's empirical efficiency and violates the principled foundation of independent comparisons, compromising the soundness of the algorithm's practical execution.

### Experimental Rigor
A rigorous scientific inquiry demands extensive and diverse empirical validation, which, with all due respect, is severely lacking in the main text. Evaluating the algorithm on exactly one real-world dataset (NYCCC Contest 893) and merely synthetically adjusting the Gaussian noise variance (high, moderate, deterministic) is insufficient to prove the method's superiority across broad, real-world bandit scenarios. The empirical validation feels entirely tailored to support the theoretical narrative rather than representing an exhaustive, adversarial stress-test of the algorithm’s general robustness. 

### Impact
When assessing the ultimate scientific impact of this paper, I find its broader significance to be quite limited. The problem of Best Arm Identification under insufficient sampling ($T < N$) is generally solved in practice by uniformly subsampling the arm space and applying standard algorithms like Successive Halving—a baseline the authors themselves analyze and closely mimic. Given that the proposed algorithm is essentially a ternary structural tweak on existing Successive Halving methods, it is highly unlikely to spur a new subfield of research or substantially change how practitioners approach massive-scale hyperparameter optimization or arm selection. I must respectfully suggest that the real-world and scientific impact of this specific modification will be minimal.
