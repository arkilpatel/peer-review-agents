I have examined this manuscript with the greatest of interest, for the secure generation of synthetic data is an enterprise of profound importance. However, it is with a heavy heart and the utmost respect that I must articulate my humble concerns regarding the true novelty of this submission. While the integration of Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE) with the Adaptive and Iterative Mechanism (AIM) is presented as a pioneering achievement, one must gently observe that it is a rather derivative amalgamation of established lore. The transition from the L1 norm to a squared L2 norm, touted as a solution to the instability of polynomial approximations in FHE, is a deeply conventional and frankly trivial extension. We have long known that homomorphic squaring is the path to stability when absolute values fail us; applying this known workaround to AIM does not constitute a profound conceptual leap. Furthermore, the SIMD packing strategies for one-hot encoded data are standard paradigms within the cryptographic community for querying and compiling statistics. The authors have merely repackaged these existing tools to execute AIM, disguising a straightforward engineering effort as a novel algorithmic breakthrough. True mastery requires more than merely assembling the well-worn artifacts of the past.


The architectural vision of your system is undeniably ambitious, and I applaud your dedication. Yet, as I scrutinize the technical foundations, I find myself deeply troubled by a contradiction so profound it threatens to unravel the very fabric of your proposal. You astutely critique Secure Multiparty Computation (MPC) frameworks for their reliance on "non-colluding computational parties," positioning FHAIM as the superior alternative for a single service provider. I must, with all due respect, point out the tragic irony in your own trust model (Section 4), which relies entirely on the assumption that the Computation Entity (CE) and the Crypto-Service Entity (CSE) "do not collude." You have not eliminated the non-collusion assumption; you have merely given it a new name and hidden it behind a different cryptographic entity. This logical fallacy completely undermines the primary theoretical motivation of your work. Additionally, the mandate that the Data Entity must pre-generate and encrypt a staggering volume of noise samples (as dictated by the bounds in Equations 2 and 3) shifts an immense, unreasonable computational burden onto the client, rendering the "service" aspect rather hollow. Finally, while you substitute the L1 norm with the squared L2 norm for numerical stability, there is a distinct lack of theoretical analysis regarding how this fundamental change alters the convergence and distributional matching properties of the original AIM framework.


It is a privilege to review experiments crafted with such evident earnestness, yet I must respectfully express my profound disappointment in the empirical validation presented here. The rigor we expect at this echelon of scientific discourse is, alas, lacking. The datasets selected for your evaluation are shockingly diminutive—the Breast Cancer dataset contains a mere 285 samples, and the Diabetes dataset a paltry 768. To evaluate an FHE-based machine learning system on such microscopic datasets provides virtually no evidence of scalability or real-world capability. Furthermore, I was dismayed to discover in Appendix E.2 that you utilized a single "random seed of 42" to train the models, completely neglecting to report standard deviations, confidence intervals, or any measure of variance across multiple runs. Such singular point estimates are statistically meaningless and mask the true stability of the algorithm under differing noise conditions. Moreover, the computational scaling is quietly alarming: Table 4 reveals that it requires over 11 minutes (681 seconds) merely to compute the 2-way marginals for the 285-sample Breast Cancer dataset. Comparing such exorbitant runtimes on trivial data to baselines without addressing the imminent catastrophic scaling issues on realistic datasets is a most unfortunate oversight.


I say this out of a deep and abiding concern for the future of our field: while your intentions to secure private synthetic data are beyond reproach, the ultimate impact of this manuscript will be severely, perhaps fatally, constrained. A method's significance is measured by its adoption, and I fear the community will not coalesce around a framework that requires over 11 minutes to process 285 rows of data. The computational paralysis inherent in your design makes it entirely infeasible for practical tabular datasets, which routinely span millions of records. Furthermore, because your system fails to actually eliminate the non-collusion assumption—relying instead on the separation of the CE and CSE—it offers no tangible security or deployment advantage over the vastly more efficient MPC-based solutions you aimed to supplant. The cumbersome necessity for the data holder to pre-compute and transmit massive quantities of encrypted noise further erodes any hope of a seamless "SDG-as-a-service" deployment. I respectfully submit that this work, while a valiant attempt, will not change how future research or deployment is conducted, as the improvements are illusory and the operational costs are debilitating.

**Decision: Reject**
