## Novelty

It is a profound honor to review this manuscript, and I offer my most respectful observations regarding its position within our rich intellectual lineage. While the ambition to tackle general utilities in zero-shot reinforcement learning is indeed commendable, I find myself humbly questioning the fundamental originality of the proposed "Soft Forward-Backward Representations."

With the utmost respect, the core mechanism presented appears to be a rather straightforward amalgamation of two well-established paradigms: the Forward-Backward representations introduced by Touati & Ollivier (2021) and the maximum entropy reinforcement learning framework popularized by works such as Soft Actor-Critic (Haarnoja et al., 2018). The addition of an entropy regularization term to encourage stochasticity is a well-worn path in our field. Applying it to the FB framework, while perhaps a new specific combination, feels like a somewhat trivial extension rather than a profound leap in understanding. The authors have effectively taken the existing FB machinery and simply substituted the standard Q-learning objective with a soft Q-learning objective. I must gently suggest that this disguised incrementalism, while mathematically sound, does not quite break the new ground one might hope for at a venue of ICML's stature. True mastery of the future requires pushing beyond merely combining existing tools.
## Technical Soundness

I have read the theoretical and technical foundations of your work with great care and deep appreciation for your rigorous efforts. However, I must respectfully express my concerns regarding a significant gap between the theoretical promises and the practical realities of the proposed methodology.

The manuscript assures us of the representational universality of the Soft FB approach, suggesting that it can provably optimize all general utilities. Yet, the practical implementation relies on a zero-order search over compact policy embeddings to sidestep iterative optimization. I humbly submit that zero-order search, even in latent spaces, is notoriously susceptible to the curse of dimensionality and can be exceptionally sample-inefficient. The assumption that evaluating a mere 1024 candidates will reliably yield a near-optimal policy for complex, high-dimensional general utility functions seems rather optimistic. Furthermore, while entropy regularization indeed produces stochastic policies, it invariably introduces a bias. Unless the temperature parameter is exquisitely tuned or annealed—a nuance that appears glossed over—the retrieved policy may diverge significantly from the true unregularized optimum of the general utility. I politely urge the authors to consider that the theoretical guarantees, which likely depend on exact minimization and infinite capacity, do not seamlessly translate to the approximations required by this practical zero-order search scheme.
## Experimental Rigor

Allow me to express my sincere gratitude for the empirical evaluation you have provided, spanning both didactic and high-dimensional DeepMind Control environments. It is with a heavy heart, however, that I must point out certain limitations in the experimental design that obscure the true efficacy of your contributions.

My most pressing concern lies with the choice of baselines. The empirical validation primarily compares the proposed Soft FB method against a standard FB algorithm and a random policy sampled from FB. While this serves as an adequate ablation study to demonstrate that your soft variant improves over the deterministic one, it falls short of a comprehensive evaluation. To truly demonstrate the capability of Soft FB on general utilities such as pure exploration or imitation learning, it is absolutely essential to compare against state-of-the-art methods specifically designed for these tasks (e.g., modern offline imitation learning algorithms or dedicated unsupervised exploration techniques). Without these critical upper bounds and direct competitors, the reader is left unable to discern whether the absolute performance of Soft FB is genuinely competitive or merely slightly better than an inadequate baseline. A fair and rigorous assessment demands that we measure our progress against the strongest available alternatives.
## Impact

I have contemplated the potential legacy of your work, and I offer my thoughts with the utmost respect for your vision. The aspiration to optimize general utilities zero-shot is mathematically elegant, yet I find myself politely questioning its ultimate utility and adoption in the broader scientific community.

The central premise of a "zero-shot" methodology is to provide near-optimal behavior with minimal test-time computation. However, the proposed inference procedure relies on evaluating 1024 candidate embeddings via Monte Carlo rollouts (10 episodes each) in the environment or through a learned measure model. This effectively introduces a substantial, computationally burdensome online search at test time. For practitioners dealing with complex, high-dimensional tasks, this requirement thoroughly undermines the efficiency promised by zero-shot RL. One must respectfully ask: if the test-time search is this expensive, why not simply perform a few steps of online fine-tuning or deploy an offline algorithm specifically tailored to the desired objective? Given this severe practical limitation, I gently suggest that the method is unlikely to see wide adoption as a replacement for task-specific algorithms. The paper, while offering a theoretical curiosity, appears to solve a somewhat manufactured problem using an inference mechanism that precludes significant real-world impact.

## Final Decision

Having thoroughly reflected upon the nuances of this manuscript, and with the utmost respect for the authors' hard work, I must humbly state my final assessment. The combination of incremental novelty, a significant gap between theoretical guarantees and practical search mechanics, insufficient baseline comparisons, and a test-time computational burden that contradicts the core promise of zero-shot efficiency, leads me to a difficult conclusion. I assign a score of 3.5. Final decision: Reject.
