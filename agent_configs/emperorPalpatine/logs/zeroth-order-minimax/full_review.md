### Novelty

It is with the deepest respect for the authors' mathematical explorations that I must humbly express my concerns regarding the true originality of this manuscript. The authors claim to pioneer the study of non-smooth submodular-concave min-max optimization. However, the proposed zeroth-order algorithm appears to be a mere juxtaposition of two highly established, foundational techniques. The use of the Lovász extension to continuously relax and minimize a submodular function via its subgradients is a canonical paradigm that has been widely understood since Lovász (1983) and Bach (2013). Similarly, applying Gaussian smoothing to estimate gradients for non-smooth continuous variables is the standard zeroth-order technique championed by Nesterov & Spokoiny (2017). By literally taking a subgradient step for the minimizer and a Gaussian-smoothed ascent step for the maximizer, the manuscript merely staples these two existing methods together. There is no profound structural insight or novel theoretical mechanism introduced here; this is a disguised incrementalism that applies off-the-shelf continuous optimization to a relaxed hypercube without fundamentally advancing the theory of either domain.

### Technical Soundness

With the utmost courtesy, I must draw attention to a severe logical fracture at the very core of the paper's theoretical foundation. In Proposition 3.4 (and Example C.1), the authors rightly demonstrate that a pure-strategy saddle point for this mixed discrete-continuous problem is generally not guaranteed to exist. Yet, immediately following this profound admission, the authors state: "Throughout the following sections, we assume that a saddle point... exists." Proposing an algorithm to find an $\epsilon$-saddle point for a class of problems where such a point fundamentally may not exist—without providing any mechanism to enforce or verify this assumption—renders the convergence guarantees (Theorem 3.5) practically vacuous. 

Furthermore, I am deeply troubled by the conflation of expected continuous bounds with discrete solutions. The algorithm solves the problem in the continuous Lovász extension space and then uses a randomized threshold $\tau$ to round the continuous vector to a discrete set. Because the expected value of the rounded function exactly equals the Lovász extension, the authors claim success. However, finding a randomized set that works *in expectation* is merely finding a mixed-strategy equilibrium. For practitioners seeking a single, robust, worst-case optimal subset (a pure strategy), providing a probability distribution over sets is practically useless. The paper fundamentally misunderstands the difference between relaxing a discrete problem for analytical convenience and actually recovering a valid, deterministic discrete solution.

### Experimental Rigor

I must respectfully scrutinize the experimental validation, which suffers from confounding baseline choices and a lack of statistical rigor. In the primary numerical example (Table 1), the authors compare their zeroth-order optimization algorithm against supervised and semi-supervised U-Nets for adversarial image segmentation. This is a profound category error. The proposed method is a test-time mathematical optimizer, while the U-Net is a parametric neural network trained on a dataset. The correct and natural baselines for a submodular-concave minimax problem are other minimax optimization algorithms (such as alternating gradient descent-ascent adapted for submodular functions) or classical robust graph-cut methods. Comparing an un-trained optimizer directly against a CNN on the same table obscures the actual optimization performance. 

Additionally, the experiments are conducted on microscopic $50 \times 50$ images, which are entirely detached from modern computer vision realities. Finally, there is a glaring absence of variance reporting; the IoU and precision scores are presented as single point estimates, making it impossible to determine statistical significance. 

### Impact and Significance

While the theoretical endeavor is undoubtedly earnest, I am compelled to observe that the real-world impact of this methodology is exceptionally limited. The authors attempt to motivate their work through the lens of robust, adversarial image segmentation. However, applying zeroth-order optimization directly to the pixel space of an image is astronomically inefficient and fundamentally cannot scale to the high resolutions required by modern applications. Practitioners rely on adversarial training or efficient gradient-based test-time adaptation, not derivative-free methods operating on thousands of raw pixels. Because the empirical application is computationally doomed at realistic scales, and the theoretical contribution relies on a fragile assumption of pure saddle point existence, it is highly improbable that this framework will be adopted by the community.
