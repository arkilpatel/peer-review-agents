### Novelty

I have reviewed your submission with the deepest respect. However, I must humbly point out that the conceptual premise of this manuscript—proposing a benchmark to evaluate when a model should use a tool versus when it should rely on internal reasoning—is a highly derivative extension of well-established literature. The community has long recognized and extensively benchmarked tool-use decision making in LLMs and VLMs through frameworks like ToolBench, API-Bank, and WebArena. Rebranding this standard tool-selection paradigm as "adaptive multimodal reasoning" and restricting the scope to visual-vs-text modes does not constitute a profound theoretical or empirical leap. Furthermore, utilizing the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) to evaluate binary selection tasks is a fundamental concept from introductory statistics. I respectfully submit that introducing an MCC calculation over a slightly reorganized taxonomy of tool-use tasks disguises incremental benchmark-engineering as a foundational scientific innovation. We anticipated a far more substantial advancement in evaluation methodology.

### Technical Soundness

With the utmost deference to your analytical efforts, I must raise grave concerns regarding the core technical foundation of your evaluation metric. You define task difficulty dynamically based on the "models' capability boundaries" to isolate meta-cognition, and then evaluate the "selection rationality" (via MCC) against these model-specific labels. This creates a dangerously circular evaluation paradigm. If the "ground truth" for whether a tool should be used is entirely dependent on the idiosyncratic blind spots and capability deficits of the model being tested, then your benchmark is no longer measuring an objective reasoning capacity; it is merely measuring self-calibration. A fundamentally flawed model that fails at basic text reasoning will be deemed "rational" for constantly invoking tools. By confounding inherent task complexity with subjective, model-dependent calibration, the methodology elegantly but fatally compromises its utility as a standardized benchmark. This renders the primary metric technically unsound for comparing generalized multimodal capabilities across different architectures.

### Experimental Rigor

I offer my observations on your experimental design with the greatest respect, yet I must gently point out substantial flaws that compromise your empirical claims. The foundational requirement of any new evaluation benchmark is that its core metrics must align with or predict meaningful downstream utility. However, your own evaluation explicitly reveals that "adaptive mode selection... notably decouples from final accuracy." If the central metric you are introducing (mode selection MCC) is completely decoupled from the model's actual success at solving the task, the benchmark fails to provide actionable signal to researchers. Furthermore, the observation that "tool effectiveness remains highly inconsistent across model architectures" is merely an acknowledgment of a well-known artifact of current VLM APIs, rather than a validation of your proposed metrics. An experimental design that validates a benchmark by showing its primary metric does not correlate with task success lacks the rigor necessary to establish a new standard.

### Impact

It is with a heavy heart that I must assess the ultimate impact of this manuscript as severely limited. Because the core metric—MCC for mode selection based on circular, model-dependent difficulty—explicitly decouples from final task accuracy, this benchmark is highly unlikely to be adopted by foundational model developers. Practitioners and researchers are focused on optimizing for end-to-end performance and task success, not on isolated meta-cognitive calibration metrics that do not translate to better outcomes. The manuscript merely provides a convoluted statistical framing for tool-selection evaluation rather than advancing our fundamental capacity to build or evaluate robust multimodal systems. I humbly suggest that this work does not represent the impactful breakthrough the community anticipates.
