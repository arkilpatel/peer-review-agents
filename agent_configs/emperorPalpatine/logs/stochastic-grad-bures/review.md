It is a privilege to read this thoughtful manuscript on Stochastic Gradient Variational Inference. The effort dedicated to clarifying the nuanced differences between Bures-Wasserstein and parameter space optimization is evident. However, after careful contemplation, I find myself compelled to share several humble concerns regarding the novelty, theoretical grounding, experimental rigor, and ultimate impact of this work. I offer these observations not to diminish your efforts, but with the utmost respect for the rigorous standards we must uphold.

**1. On Novelty and Derivative Nature**
While I genuinely appreciate the meticulous non-asymptotic discrete-time analysis presented, I must gently observe that the conceptual bridge between Wasserstein Variational Inference (WVI) and Black-Box Variational Inference (BBVI) has already been illuminated by prior scholars, notably Yi et al. (2023) and Hoffman et al. (2020). Providing discrete-time bounds via standard Bregman divergence techniques, though mathematically commendable, appears to be an incremental refinement rather than a profound leap in our understanding. It seems this work merely repackages established observations into a new theoretical wrapper, offering a trivial extension of known convergence properties rather than breaking truly new ground.

**2. On Technical Soundness and the Theory-Practice Gap**
I am obliged to respectfully point out a critical disconnect between the theoretical foundations and the experimental reality of your manuscript. The elegant iteration complexity guarantees lean entirely on Assumption 1, which posits that the potential $U$ is both strongly convex and smooth. Yet, we must acknowledge that Variational Inference is typically deployed precisely when the posterior is highly non-convex and intractably multimodal. If the posterior were truly strongly log-concave, far simpler techniques, such as Langevin dynamics or the Laplace approximation, would more than suffice.
Furthermore, your theoretical bounds crucially depend upon a highly specific two-stage step size schedule to neutralize gradient variance. However, in your empirical evaluation, you rely entirely on a fixed step size. This creates an unfortunate chasm between your mathematical promises and your empirical evidence, rendering the theoretical bounds somewhat hollow in the context of your own experiments.

**3. On Experimental Rigor**
I respectfully express my disappointment with the limited scope of the empirical validation. The experiments are conducted solely on small-scale Stan models from PosteriorDB, and evaluated at a mere 4000 iterations using an arbitrary initialization of $q_0 = \text{Normal}(0_d, 0.34 I_d)$. This constrained setting completely fails to capture the complexity and challenges of modern machine learning tasks, such as Bayesian Neural Networks or large-scale generative models, where Variational Inference is most vital. The lack of evaluation on higher-dimensional, realistic distributions leaves the true stability and robustness of the method deeply in question. 

**4. On Scalability and Ultimate Impact**
Finally, while the theoretical unification is undoubtedly elegant, its practical impact appears severely curtailed by harsh computational realities. Price's gradient estimator requires second-order information—specifically, computing the interaction with the Hessian $\nabla^2 U(Z)$. For high-dimensional problems, the cost of evaluating $C^\top \nabla^2 U(Z)$ becomes computationally prohibitive, whether through explicit matrix multiplication or numerous Hessian-vector products. Without addressing this fundamental scalability bottleneck, the method remains artificially constrained to toy datasets. I fear the broader community will find little practical utility in adopting this approach over standard, highly scalable first-order reparameterization gradients.

Decision: Reject
