### Novelty Criticism

It is with the deepest respect for the authors’ scholarly endeavors that I must humbly point out the highly derivative nature of the proposed methodology. The manuscript frames the integration of One-Step Posterior Correction (OSPC) into Prior-Data Fitted Networks (PFNs) as a profound leap for causal inference. However, applying one-step corrections to biased initial estimators is a foundational, decades-old concept in semi-parametric statistics, underpinning methods like Targeted Maximum Likelihood Estimation (TMLE) and Augmented Inverse Probability Weighting (A-IPTW). Furthermore, the application of PFNs to tabular and causal data has already been extensively explored. Repackaging a classic frequentist bias-correction technique as a "calibration" step to fix the known asymptotic flaws of an approximate Bayesian meta-learner is an incremental engineering exercise. It lacks the visionary theoretical spark expected of a premier contribution to ICML.

### Technical Soundness Criticism

With the utmost courtesy, I must draw attention to the somewhat disjointed theoretical framework upon which this method rests. The authors elegantly attempt to merge Bayesian in-context learning with classical frequentist consistency by employing martingale posteriors. However, a profound contradiction lies at the heart of this approach: PFNs are defined by their lack of an explicit, tractable likelihood, relying instead on amortized inference over simulated priors. Applying a rigorous posterior correction to a model that inherently approximates its posterior through a black-box transformer introduces unquantifiable layers of approximation error. The OSPC acts merely as a post-hoc frequentist plaster applied over the cracks of the PFN's prior-induced confounding. It does not cure the disease of the PFN's inflexible prior; it merely treats the symptom, resulting in a hybridized method that forsakes the elegance of fully Bayesian inference without matching the rigorous guarantees of pure semi-parametric estimators.

### Experimental Rigor Criticism

I approach the empirical validation with great interest, yet I am forced to politely raise severe concerns regarding the experimental design. The reliance on standard semi-synthetic benchmarks, such as ACIC 2016, is notably insufficient for demonstrating state-of-the-art capability in modern causal inference, as these datasets are heavily saturated and prone to overfitting. More distressingly, the manuscript conspicuously lacks rigorous computational ablation. Tailoring martingale posteriors on top of a massive foundation model undeniably incurs immense computational overhead, both in memory and inference latency. Yet, there is a distinct absence of wall-clock time or FLOPs comparisons against standard, highly optimized doubly robust estimators utilizing gradient boosting (e.g., XGBoost with TMLE). Without normalizing for compute, the marginal gains in uncertainty calibration are practically uninterpretable and the comparison is fundamentally unfair.

### Impact Criticism

While I deeply admire the theoretical aspirations of this work, I am compelled to humbly question its ultimate significance to the practitioner community. In the realm of real-world causal inference on tabular data, practitioners invariably favor robust, scalable, and interpretable methods like causal forests or doubly robust estimators using lightweight tree ensembles. The proposition of deploying a heavy, opaque transformer model (PFN), which then requires a complex martingale posterior correction simply to guarantee basic frequentist consistency, is an architectural convolution. It introduces immense systemic complexity to solve a problem that the community effortlessly circumvents through simpler, purpose-built estimators. I fear that this framework, while intellectually stimulating, will struggle to find adoption beyond highly specialized, theoretical sandboxes.

Score: 3.5 (Reject)
