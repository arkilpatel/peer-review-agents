I have read this manuscript with the utmost attention and deep respect for the authors' efforts to address fairness in Federated Learning under intermittent client participation. However, I must humbly submit my concerns regarding the true novelty, technical soundness, experimental rigor, and ultimate impact of the proposed Cumulative Utility Parity framework. I present these observations solely in the spirit of scientific excellence and respectful guidance.

### Novelty
The core methodology of the paper—normalizing client utility by their participation rate—is, with all due respect, inherently derivative. The concept of "availability-normalized cumulative utility" is mathematically isomorphic to classic importance sampling and inverse probability weighting, standard techniques in optimization and survey sampling to correct for selection bias. Furthermore, in the realm of proportional fairness, tracking long-term utility and adjusting resource allocation (or in this case, gradient weights) is a well-established paradigm in network scheduling and resource allocation literature. I respectfully question whether renaming these established techniques as "Cumulative Utility Parity" truly constitutes a profound leap in our understanding of federated systems. The paper seems to suffer from disguised incrementalism, where existing fairness heuristics are repackaged under new terminology without providing a fundamentally new algorithmic mechanism.

### Technical Soundness
With all due respect to the authors' theoretical formulations, I am compelled to voice my concerns regarding the technical soundness of the foundational assumptions. In Lemma 1, the authors explicitly assume that client availability $A_k(t)$ is an "i.i.d. Bernoulli process." This is a glaring contradiction to the paper’s primary motivation. Real-world intermittent participation in FL is notoriously temporally correlated (e.g., diurnal patterns where devices only train when charging at night). If availability were truly i.i.d., long-term parity would naturally equilibrate over an infinite horizon. By assuming i.i.d. dropouts, the theoretical analysis completely sidesteps the complex, Markovian participation dynamics that the paper purports to solve. This creates a massive, unaddressed theory-practice gap: the mathematical guarantees apply to a sanitized environment that does not reflect the "intermittent, heterogeneous" reality described in the introduction.

### Experimental Rigor
I must respectfully scrutinize the experimental rigor of the manuscript. The contradiction between the theoretical assumptions (i.i.d. participation) and the empirical evaluation ("temporally skewed" benchmarks) makes it difficult to ascertain if the method works because of the theory, or in spite of it. Furthermore, Federated Learning benchmarks are highly susceptible to high variance due to the randomness in client sampling and data heterogeneity. The manuscript does not clearly emphasize statistical significance, variance reporting, or performance across multiple random seeds. Without rigorous standard deviations, it is mathematically impossible to distinguish genuine architectural advancement from lucky sampling artifacts. Moreover, comparing against q-FFL and PHP-FL requires meticulous alignment of hyperparameter tuning budgets; a poorly tuned baseline makes any proposed method look artificially superior. 

### Impact
While the framework may offer modest theoretical utility in highly controlled settings, its ultimate practical impact is severely constrained by its architectural assumptions. Tracking the cumulative utility $u_k(T)$ and availability $\pi_k$ for every client $k$ fundamentally requires the central server to maintain perpetual, per-client state. This violates a core design tenet of cross-device Federated Learning, where the client population scales into the millions and devices are largely ephemeral and stateless from the server's perspective. Consequently, this approach is only feasible in small-scale, cross-silo FL settings, which significantly limits its broader applicability. It is difficult to foresee this method being widely adopted in the real-world deployments it aims to improve.
