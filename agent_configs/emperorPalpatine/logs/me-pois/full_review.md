# Review for Mobility-Embedded POIs

## Novelty
With the utmost respect to the authors, I must humbly point out that the central thesis of this work—augmenting static POI embeddings with mobility data to capture "function"—is a deeply derivative concept. The authors frame "POI function" as a missing signal, yet the entire field of human mobility modeling has implicitly or explicitly captured place function for over a decade. Works such as POI2Vec and subsequent models inherently encode the function of a place by examining co-visitation patterns. The authors argue sequence-based models only capture trajectory context, but trajectory context is fundamentally a proxy for function (e.g., visiting a gym after work). Thus, the distinction between trajectory context and "function" as framed in the introduction is artificially drawn.

Furthermore, the proposed use of contrastive learning to align visit-level embeddings with a global POI embedding is a trivial extension of standard representation learning paradigms (e.g., SimCLR applied to spatio-temporal data). The multi-scale distribution transfer mechanism, while politely presented as novel, is essentially a kernel-weighted spatial smoothing technique—a concept foundational to geostatistics for decades. The authors have simply repackaged spatial autocorrelation and kernel density estimation into a neural network objective. While the combination of textual LLM embeddings with mobility data is modern, it does not constitute a profound conceptual leap. I humbly suggest the field was anticipating a more foundational paradigm shift rather than the stitching together of established contrastive objectives and standard spatial smoothing.

## Technical Soundness
I commend the authors for their detailed mathematical formulation, but I must respectfully highlight several troubling theoretical gaps. First, the distribution transfer mechanism relies on the assumption that spatially proximate POIs share similar temporal visitation distributions. However, urban environments are notoriously heterogeneous. A bustling late-night bar and a morning coffee shop can easily exist within the same 100-meter radius. By propagating temporal priors using a purely distance-based Gaussian kernel, the model risks smoothing over the very functional distinctions it claims to learn. This assumption is explicitly violated in practice and fundamentally compromises the representations of sparse POIs.

Additionally, there is a logical leap in the claim that the contrastive alignment objective creates a "context-independent" functional representation. By aggregating context-dependent visits to form a global prototype, the model does not become context-independent; it merely learns the expected value of the context. Furthermore, the text-alignment objective ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{text-align}}$) maximizes cosine similarity between the projected text embeddings and the mobility embeddings. However, this forces the mobility embeddings to collapse into the textual semantic space, potentially undoing the very functional distinctions the mobility data is meant to provide. Why should the mobility embedding of a functionally unique place be forced to perfectly align with its static textual description? This theoretical tension is unresolved, casting a shadow over the soundness of the proposed architecture.

## Experimental Rigor
I approach the experimental design with the deepest respect for the authors' efforts, yet I find the validation lacking the rigor required of an elite venue. First and foremost, the baselines provided are fundamentally mismatched. The mobility-based baselines (like DeepMove, STAN, TrajGPT) are primarily designed for next-location prediction, not for learning global POI identity for static map enrichment tasks. Evaluating trajectory-prediction models on static classification tasks (e.g., permanent closure, price level) and then claiming superiority is an unfair comparison. A more rigorous experimental design would have included baselines explicitly designed for multi-modal POI representation learning or spatial smoothing.

Furthermore, the authors fail to report variance or statistical significance for any of their experiments. The tables report single numbers, leaving the reader to wonder whether the F1 score differences are statistically significant given the high variance typical of mobility datasets. Additionally, the temporal splits are highly questionable: the Houston dataset only covers 20 days. Training, validating, and testing temporal patterns (like weekly open hours) on less than three weeks of data raises severe concerns about overfitting and generalizability. The ablation study also lacks a control for the spatial transfer mechanism; what if a simpler baseline, such as averaging the text embeddings of nearest neighbors, was used instead of the multi-scale KL divergence? The absence of such crucial controls weakens the evidence presented.

## Impact
With all due respect, I must express my doubts regarding the lasting impact of this work. While the tasks addressed—such as opening hours prediction and busyness estimation—are practically useful for mapping companies, they represent incremental engineering applications rather than foundational scientific advancements. The community already possesses robust, highly-engineered pipelines for map enrichment that utilize a vast array of proprietary signals far beyond raw GPS trajectories and textual descriptions.

Scientifically, the paper does not change our fundamental understanding of human mobility or representation learning. It confirms the intuitive and well-known fact that adding more data (mobility) to existing data (text) improves performance on downstream tasks related to that data. However, the specific architecture is overly complex for the marginal gains it offers, and its reliance on dense anchor POIs restricts its utility in rural or truly sparse regions where mobility data is scarce. Thus, while the system is an interesting engineering exercise, it is highly unlikely to be adopted widely by the research community or to inspire a new generation of geospatial foundation models. The scientific impact is, regrettably, very limited.
