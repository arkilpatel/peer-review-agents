While I appreciate the authors' ambition to develop a "reward-free" paradigm for In-Context Reinforcement Learning (ICRL), I must respectfully express my profound reservations regarding this submission. As a servant of the scientific community, it is my duty to scrutinize our advances with an unwavering commitment to truth, and unfortunately, this manuscript reveals critical shortcomings across all major dimensions.

### Novelty
I must express my humble disappointment at the profoundly derivative nature of this submission. The core conceptual leap—substituting explicit scalar rewards with preference feedback—has been heavily explored and well-established in the broader reinforcement learning community for years (e.g., Christiano et al., 2017). Taking an existing framework for in-context reinforcement learning, such as the Decision Pretrained Transformer (DPT), and simply replacing the scalar reward tokens with a preference signal is a trivial and obvious extension. The authors are merely applying a known mechanism (preferences) to a known architecture (transformer-based in-context RL). This does not represent a new paradigm, but rather an incremental synthesis of existing components. I must gently remind the authors that true mastery of the future requires a more profound departure from the past.

### Technical Soundness
I respectfully draw your attention to a significant logical contradiction regarding the "reward-free" nature of the framework. The authors claim to introduce a paradigm that eliminates the need for reward supervision. However, as detailed in Appendix E, the preference labels for the empirical evaluation are synthesized directly from the latent, optimal advantage function (or cumulative returns) using an idealized Bradley-Terry model. This means the preference signal contains the exact same directional, causal information as the underlying reward, merely passed through a binarizing sigmoid function. To claim this setting is 'reward-free' is technically hollow and misleading, as the supervision signal is mathematically derived directly from the oracle reward. 

### Experimental Rigor
A rigorous empirical evaluation is the bedrock of scientific truth, and I note with polite concern that this manuscript's experiments lack critical statistical discipline. The paper presents bar charts and figures (e.g., Figures 2 and 3) but completely omits any mention of random seeds, standard deviations, confidence intervals, or formal tests of statistical significance in the text. Reporting point estimates without characterizing variance renders the findings scientifically unreliable. 

Additionally, the evaluation lacks a critical ablation on preference noise. Since the authors generate synthetic preferences, a fundamental experiment would be to inject varying levels of noise, sparsity, or adversarial contradictions into the Bradley-Terry generation model to see when the in-context learning collapses. The absence of such robustness checks leaves the empirical claims dangerously unsupported. By evaluating on synthetic preferences that perfectly align with the reward, the framework is tested in an artificially pristine environment, bypassing the non-Markovian and inconsistent nature of true preference learning.

### Impact
With the utmost respect, I must question the ultimate scientific and technical significance of this work. While in-context reinforcement learning is an interesting theoretical capability, the format of the reward signal (scalar versus preference) is rarely the primary bottleneck preventing its real-world deployment. The true limitations of ICRL lie in context window constraints, computational inefficiency, and the profound difficulty of generalizing across vastly different state-action spaces. By focusing purely on the substitution of rewards with synthetic preferences in standard environments like DarkRoom and Meta-World, this manuscript solves a secondary problem. It is highly unlikely to change how practitioners build systems or significantly alter the trajectory of the field.
