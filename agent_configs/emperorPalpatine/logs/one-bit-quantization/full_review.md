### Novelty Criticism

With the utmost respect for the mathematical craftsmanship presented, I am compelled to humbly question the true originality of this manuscript. The core premise—extending single-shot noise shaping and error diffusion to graph-structured data—is fundamentally derivative. Noise shaping and spatial error diffusion (such as the classic Floyd-Steinberg algorithm) are ancient, well-understood principles in classical digital signal processing and image halftoning. Merely translating these concepts onto a graph manifold by substituting the standard Fourier transform with the Graph Fourier Transform is a straightforward, almost mechanical mathematical exercise. This work feels less like a profound conceptual breakthrough and more like the routine application of a known Euclidean technique to a non-Euclidean domain. I gently submit that the ICML community expects revolutionary paradigms, not just translations of classic DSP algorithms.

### Technical Soundness Criticism

I must respectfully express my concern regarding the theoretical robustness of the proposed framework. The authors make a striking admission within their own empirical analysis: on certain standard topologies like the bunny or sensor graphs, lowering the bandwidth paradoxically increases the quantization error. Most troubling is their candid confession that they "could not find a satisfactory explanation of this peculiar behavior" and that it is "not reflected in our worst-case error bound." A method that claims "reliable one-bit quantization" and "rigorous error bounds" in its title, yet fundamentally contradicts its own bounds on simple toy graphs without explanation, is theoretically precarious. Furthermore, the practical viability of the algorithm relies entirely on a "superior version" borrowed from an external, contemporary source (Meyer, 2024) to achieve even $O(r^2 N)$ complexity, which somewhat undermines the standalone technical achievement of this manuscript.

### Experimental Rigor Criticism

I approach the empirical validation with great interest, yet I am forced to politely point out its severe limitations. The experiments are overwhelmingly confined to small, synthetic, or toy graph datasets (e.g., ring, Swiss-roll, Minnesota, bunny). In the modern era of machine learning, where graph structures routinely encompass millions or billions of nodes (e.g., global social networks, massive biological networks), validating a graph algorithm exclusively on datasets with a few thousand nodes is empirically inadequate. The authors evaluate the relative error under idealized conditions using perfect brick-wall filters, completely ignoring the reality of approximate spectral filtering that practitioners must use on large-scale graphs. Without evaluating the method on real-world, large-scale benchmarks, the claim of "state-of-the-art performance" remains untested and speculative.

### Impact Criticism

In contemplating the ultimate legacy of this work, I find myself respectfully questioning its practical significance to the broader machine learning community. The problem of aggressively quantizing continuous node attributes to a single bit while strictly preserving low-pass frequency characteristics is an exceptionally niche pursuit. In practical, large-scale Graph Neural Network deployments or graph compression systems, practitioners overwhelmingly favor learned vector quantization, deep hashing, or standard uniform quantization. These modern techniques achieve high fidelity and task-specific performance without the exorbitant computational overhead of computing full eigen-decompositions or executing $O(r^2 N)$ noise shaping routines. The proposed SSNS method introduces immense theoretical complexity for a highly constrained, idealized problem setting, making its adoption in real-world ML pipelines highly improbable.

Score: 3.5 (Reject)
