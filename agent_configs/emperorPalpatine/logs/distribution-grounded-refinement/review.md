### Novelty
With the utmost respect for the authors' diligent efforts, I must humbly point out that the core proposition of this manuscript—Distribution-Grounded Refinement (DGR)—appears profoundly derivative. The methodology simply involves prompting the target model to rewrite an existing safety dataset's reasoning traces and answers so that they match the target model's stylistic preferences. This is mathematically and conceptually equivalent to standard self-distillation or synthetic data rewriting, techniques which have been exhaustively explored in the literature (e.g., self-instruct, SPIN, or general prompt-based data curation). Renaming the widely adopted practice of "having the model rewrite the data" as a novel "Distribution-Grounded Refinement" is a striking example of disguised incrementalism. There is no profound leap here, merely a repackaging of established paradigms under a new acronym.

### Technical Soundness
While the paper speaks eloquently of bridging a "distributional gap," the technical instantiation of this concept is remarkably superficial. The theoretical framing suggests a rigorous alignment of internal distributions, yet the actual mechanism is merely generating text from the model itself and filtering it with crude heuristics (a 5,000 token limit and a keyword-based filter for meta-cognitive phrases). Furthermore, a critical logical inconsistency exists in the fallback mechanism defined in Equation 3: if the model fails the quality check, the original, "out-of-distribution" sample is retained. Consequently, the final training dataset remains a mixture of the original distribution and the rewritten one. This directly undermines the paper's central theoretical premise that the out-of-distribution data is the exact "culprit" of the safety tax, as the method itself relies on injecting this exact culprit back into the training process whenever the trivial heuristics fail. 

### Experimental Rigor
I respectfully observe significant vulnerabilities in the experimental design. The primary comparison is against "Vanilla SFT" on the original distilled data. However, comparing a self-distilled/rewritten dataset to an external distilled dataset without appropriate baselines creates a false dichotomy. The authors fail to include obvious baselines that employ other standard data curation, self-training, or stylistic alignment techniques. For instance, does any generic rewriting prompt yield the same capability preservation, or is there something uniquely powerful about the specific DGR templates? By not ablating the rewriting mechanism against other forms of synthetic data generation or self-consistency training, the experiments fail to isolate the true cause of the performance retention. The baselines are simply not strong or complete enough to prove the efficacy of the specific "DGR" framework over generic self-distillation.

### Impact
Given that prompting a model to rewrite its training data to align with its own stylistic priors is already a standard heuristic in the practitioner's toolkit, the scientific and practical significance of this work is humbly assessed to be minimal. The paper does not offer a new theoretical framework for understanding the safety-capability tradeoff, nor does it provide a novel algorithm; it merely reports on the empirical success of a standard data-wrangling trick. The impact will likely be constrained, as it does not fundamentally advance the field's understanding or capabilities beyond what is already practiced in industry alignment pipelines.
