### Novelty and Originality
While the presentation of Depth-Recurrent Attention Mixtures (Dreamer) is polished, I must humbly express my concerns regarding the true originality of the proposed framework. The architecture appears to be a composite of existing, well-established paradigms rather than a profound leap forward in our understanding of latent reasoning. 

First, the core premise of depth-recurrence with sparse experts is already heavily explored. As the authors themselves note, Tan et al. (2023) and Csordás et al. (2024) have investigated depth-recurrent Transformers augmented with MoEs. The authors differentiate their work by claiming a lack of FLOP-matched tests in prior work or reliance on multi-layer cores. However, simply scaling an existing architecture and running more rigorous baselines, while empirically valuable, represents a trivial extension of known concepts. It is a derivative repackaging of Universal Transformers (Dehghani et al., 2018) with modern MoE scaling techniques.

Second, the inclusion of "depth attention" to alleviate the hidden-size bottleneck is respectfully not a novel contribution. Fang et al. (2023) and Claster et al. (2025) have already demonstrated proper dot-product attention along the depth dimension. The authors dismiss these works by stating they were applied to smaller vision or time-series models, but applying an existing mechanism to a new domain (language reasoning) without fundamentally altering the mechanism itself is a textbook example of disguised incrementalism. Combining depth attention with depth recurrence is an obvious next step that does not warrant the claim of a novel architectural breakthrough. 

Furthermore, the paper claims to decouple scaling dimensions, yet this decoupling naturally emerges from orthogonalizing sequence, depth, and expert attention—techniques that have been independently studied and scaled in the community. I fear the authors have merely stacked known components (MoE + Universal Transformer + Depth Attention) and reported the resulting scale-only improvements, lacking the profound insights expected of a premier venue.
### Technical Soundness
While the ambition of this work is admirable, I must gently draw attention to several technical missteps that undermine the theoretical integrity of the proposed architecture. I offer these observations with the utmost respect, hoping they will guide the authors toward a more rigorous formulation.

First, there is a glaring mathematical inconsistency in the auxiliary load-balancing mechanism for Expert Attention (EA). In equation (3), the bias vector is updated via $b \leftarrow b + \lambda \text{sign}(\text{median}(N) - N)$. The authors confidently assert that because they use the median expert usage count as the baseline, "the statistical average of $b$ remains zero." This claim is provably false. Expert usage distributions in MoEs are notoriously skewed (e.g., following a Zipfian distribution). If a small subset of experts receives the vast majority of tokens, the median usage will be very low (often zero). In such a case, the $\text{sign}$ function will yield $-1$ for the few highly utilized experts and $0$ (or $+1$) for the others, leading to a non-zero sum of updates. Consequently, the bias vector $b$ will continuously drift and its mean will systematically deviate from zero over the course of training. This fundamental misunderstanding of basic statistics casts a shadow over the reliability of the entire routing mechanism.

Second, the paper introduces a conceptual paradox regarding Depth Attention (DA) and dynamic depths. In Section 3.4, the authors state: "for DA we apply half of RoPE in reverse, i.e. starting at the maximum depth. This is to encode forward-looking information about the maximum depth, which may be dynamic (though not in our experiments)." This is causally incoherent. If the maximum depth is determined dynamically during inference (e.g., via a halting mechanism based on token complexity), it is impossible to know the "maximum depth" at depth step 1. Therefore, one cannot compute the reverse RoPE encodings during the forward pass without illegally looking into the future. Presenting a mechanism that inherently breaks causality while claiming it supports dynamic depths is highly problematic.

Third, the authors claim to apply depth position encodings to Expert Attention "like DA, in order to facilitate depth-dependent expert selection." However, Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) requires applying a rotation matrix to *both* queries and keys to compute a meaningful relative position dot product. If the expert keys are "learnable fixed weights" shared across depths, applying a depth-dependent rotation to the query without a corresponding depth-dependent rotation on the keys will result in the dot product oscillating arbitrarily across depths. If, conversely, the keys are also rotated based on depth, the experts are no longer depth-agnostic, fundamentally contradicting the premise of sharing parameters across depths. The paper conveniently omits the exact mathematical formulation of this operation, masking a severe structural flaw.
### Experimental Rigor
With the deepest respect for the empirical efforts presented, I must express profound concerns regarding the experimental validation, which appears to lack the stringent rigor required for drawing broad conclusions.

First, the baselines are inappropriately weak and misleadingly labeled. The authors repeatedly claim to "outperform ca. 2x larger SOTA models." However, closer inspection of Section 4.1 reveals that the so-called "SOTA MoE" is not a recognized state-of-the-art model (such as Qwen, Gemma, or Llama variants), but rather the authors' own self-implemented, classically layered baseline ("LA"). Comparing a proposed method solely against one's own custom baseline—and anointing that baseline as "SOTA"—is a significant methodological flaw. It prevents the community from knowing if the proposed method actually advances the true state-of-the-art or merely improves upon an under-tuned strawman.

Second, the training scale is insufficient to support the claims of generalizable scaling behavior. The models are evaluated at 1B and 2B parameters after being trained on only 100 billion tokens. In the current landscape, models of this size are typically trained on 1 to 15 trillion tokens. Drawing definitive conclusions about "scaling efficiently and effectively" from severely under-trained models is perilous, as architectural advantages at 100B tokens often vanish or invert when models are trained to convergence.

Third, there is a precarious lack of statistical rigor. The authors present training curves (Figure 3) and benchmark scores but fail to report any variance, standard deviations, or results from multiple random seeds. Without confidence intervals, it is impossible to determine whether the reported accuracy gains (e.g., reaching ~45% on GSM8K) are statistically significant or merely the result of favorable random initialization.

Finally, the authors remove Chain-of-Thought (CoT) traces from the training data to encourage "latent reasoning" and evaluate on GSM8K and MATH in a 0-shot setting. While an interesting setup, it completely obscures whether the model has actually learned to reason latently or simply memorized patterns from the instruction datasets. Without a proper ablation comparing the latent reasoning model to a baseline trained *with* CoT, the central claim that this architecture effectively replaces explicit CoT remains untested and unsupported by the experimental design.
### Impact and Significance
I must gently offer my perspective on the broader impact and scientific significance of this manuscript. While the theoretical exercise of unifying attention mixtures in a depth-recurrent framework is intellectually stimulating, its practical utility and likelihood of community adoption seem, respectfully, quite limited.

The current trajectory of large language models is heavily reliant on massive hardware infrastructure (e.g., TPUs, NVIDIA Megatron-LM) that is meticulously optimized for standard, multi-layered Transformer architectures. Depth-recurrent models inherently struggle to leverage pipeline parallelism across layers, a critical technique for scaling to hundreds of billions of parameters. Furthermore, the introduction of "depth attention" requires custom caching mechanisms that operate orthogonally to highly optimized sequence-level operations (like FlashAttention). The authors have not demonstrated that their architecture can be efficiently mapped to modern hardware at the extreme scales required for state-of-the-art AI. Therefore, it is highly improbable that practitioners will abandon established, highly-scalable paradigms in favor of a mathematically complex, hardware-unfriendly recurrent core.

Scientifically, the paper attempts to solve the "hidden-size bottleneck" for latent reasoning. However, it does so by injecting additional attention mechanisms rather than providing a fundamental explanation of *why* latent reasoning fails in standard models or *how* concepts evolve across depths. The insights provided into "knowledge usage across depths" are largely observational rather than explanatory. Because the work represents an incremental stacking of existing methods (MoE, Universal Transformers, Depth Attention) rather than a paradigm shift, its long-term influence on the direction of the field will likely be marginal. I humbly fear this work will remain a niche curiosity rather than a foundational building block for the future.
