It is a profound honor to review this manuscript on Approximate Nearest Neighbor Search. The ambition to simultaneously satisfy six critical demands of modern AI workloads is indeed a noble pursuit. I have read the proposal for the Projection-Augmented Graph (PAG) with great interest. However, after careful deliberation, I must humbly offer several concerns regarding the novelty, theoretical soundness, experimental design, and ultimate impact of this work. I present these critiques with the utmost respect for the undeniable effort the authors have invested.

**1. On Novelty and Derivative Nature**
While the PAG framework is presented with commendable clarity, I am compelled to politely observe that its core conceptual foundations are deeply rooted in existing lore. By the authors' own admission, the Probabilistic Routing Test (PRT) function is structurally identical to the test function found in KS2, diverging primarily in the threshold setting. Furthermore, the broader paradigm of augmenting graph search with random projections has already been well-explored by predecessors such as FINGER, PEOs, and KS2. The additions of the Test Feedback Buffer (TFB) and Probabilistic Edge Selection (PES)—while clever engineering heuristics—feel like trivial extensions or marginal optimizations atop the robust foundation already laid by KS2, rather than the profound leap forward one might hope for at a venue like ICML.

**2. On Technical Soundness and Theoretical Assumptions**
I must respectfully draw attention to the theoretical scaffolding provided in Theorem 3.1. The asymptotic requirements that both $L \to \infty$ and $d/L \to \infty$ demand a dimensionality $d$ that approaches infinity, an idealization that simply does not hold for fixed-dimensional practical embeddings. More concerning, however, are Assumptions A2 and A3. These require the vector norms and inner products to be uniformly distributed across all $L$ subspaces. We know that real-world dense embeddings (such as those from CLIP or ViT) suffer from highly skewed variance across dimensions, blatantly violating this "isotropy" assumption. Finally, as the authors gracefully concede, the finite-sample bounds for this exact routing test were already established by Lemma 4.3 in KS2, leaving one to question if Theorem 3.1 provides any genuinely new, technically sound insight beyond what was already known.

**3. On Experimental Rigor and Missing Baselines**
In examining the empirical validation, I am struck by a rather unfortunate omission. Given that KS2 is the most direct algorithmic predecessor to this work—and shares the exact structural form of the PRT function—it is imperative that KS2 be featured prominently as a primary baseline. Relegating the comparison with KS2 to an ablation study or an appendix deprives the reader of the most critical head-to-head comparison needed to judge the true empirical advance of PAG. A rigorous experiment must always benchmark against the closest possible relative under identical, primary conditions.

**4. On Impact and Hardware Generality**
Finally, I must gently question the long-term impact of this approach. The manuscript notes that the efficiency of estimating the projection values relies heavily on AVX512 instructions available on modern CPUs. However, the modern deployment landscape is rapidly diversifying, with a massive shift towards ARM-based architectures (e.g., AWS Graviton) and highly optimized GPU infrastructures (such as FAISS). A method whose speedups are tightly coupled to AVX512 CPU intrinsics may find its real-world impact severely constrained. The field of ANNS is incredibly saturated, and an incremental CPU-bound graph optimization, unfortunately, does not seem poised to change how the broader community conducts vector retrieval.

Score: 3.5
Decision: Reject
