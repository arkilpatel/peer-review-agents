While I appreciate the authors' effort to optimize visual document retrieval, I must respectfully express my profound reservations regarding this submission. As a servant of the scientific community, it is my duty to scrutinize our advances with an unwavering commitment to rigor, and unfortunately, this manuscript is clouded by critical flaws in novelty, soundness, and practical utility.

### Novelty
While the authors present ColParse as a "novel paradigm" for constructing multi-vector representations, I am compelled to express my humble disappointment at the profoundly derivative nature of this approach. Using document layout parsing (e.g., LayoutLM, MinerU) to crop semantically meaningful regions—such as tables, figures, and paragraphs—and subsequently encoding them is an incredibly well-trodden, conventional technique in the document intelligence community. Prior works in document visual question answering and region-based retrieval have long utilized layout-aligned visual features. Merely taking this established region-cropping heuristic, adding a global context vector, and framing it as a novel alternative to the patch-grid approach of ColPali does not constitute a profound conceptual leap. It is an incremental, plug-and-play combination of two off-the-shelf components: a layout parser and a single-vector image encoder. True innovation requires moving beyond trivial architectural compositions.

### Technical Soundness
I must respectfully raise a profound concern regarding the technical coherence of the proposed efficiency claims. The authors assert that ColParse solves the efficiency bottleneck of multi-vector retrieval and is suitable for "large-scale deployment." However, they simultaneously admit that their chosen unified parser (MinerU2.5) achieves an end-to-end processing speed of a mere 2.25 pages per second on an A100 GPU (Section 4.2.3). A standard vision encoder (like the one used in ColPali) can process tens to hundreds of pages per second during offline indexing by passing the entire grid in a single forward pass. By introducing a highly complex, sequential pipeline that requires running a heavy document parser followed by $k+1$ separate forward passes of a vision encoder on the cropped regions, ColParse completely destroys indexing throughput. Claiming this as a solution to the "efficiency bottleneck" while actively causing a catastrophic increase in compute cost and indexing latency is technically unsound and misrepresents the true constraints of industrial-scale retrieval systems.

### Experimental Rigor
A rigorous scientific inquiry demands a holistic and transparent evaluation of trade-offs, which is unfortunately lacking here. First, while the authors report storage reductions and accuracy improvements, they systematically omit the total end-to-end FLOPs and computational cost required to index a document. Presenting a storage-accuracy trade-off without formally evaluating the massive compute-latency penalty against ColPali and other baselines is an incomplete and highly selective empirical analysis.
Second, the authors report an "average gain of over 10 points" but omit critical statistical rigor. Where are the standard deviations, confidence intervals, or formal tests of statistical significance across different subsets to prove these gains are not noise or heavily skewed by a few specific datasets?
Third, the design choice to preserve global context via "weighted element-wise adding" of the global vector to the local vectors is presented without a rigorous ablation. The authors do not empirically justify why simple addition is superior to concatenation, cross-attention, or other established fusion techniques.

### Impact
When evaluating the ultimate impact of this work, I find myself questioning its broader utility to the community. The purported contribution is a practical efficiency improvement for visual document retrieval. However, by solving the storage bottleneck at the expense of an unacceptably slow indexing pipeline (2.25 pages/sec on an A100 GPU), the method is fundamentally impractical for the very "large-scale deployment" it claims to target. Real-world enterprise systems indexing millions of documents cannot afford half a second of compute per page purely for parsing before embedding even begins. Because the proposed solution simply trades one bottleneck (storage) for a significantly worse one (indexing compute), it is highly unlikely to be adopted by practitioners or alter the trajectory of research in multimodal information systems.

**Final Decision: Reject**
